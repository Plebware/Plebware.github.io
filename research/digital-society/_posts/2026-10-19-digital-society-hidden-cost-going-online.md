# 🌐 Digital Society: Is Technology Helping Everyone?

Imagine needing to apply for a government grant, check your bank balance, look for work, or contact a family member. You reach for your cellphone, only to discover that you have no data left, your phone is too old to run the required application, or the service you need is available only online.

For some people, these are minor inconveniences. For others, they are serious obstacles that can affect their finances, employment, and everyday lives.

We often hear that technology makes life easier. And it can! We can communicate across the world, learn new skills, find work, manage our money, and access services without leaving home.

But there is another side to this story. What happens when you cannot afford the technology that modern society expects you to use?

This article explores how our increasingly digital world affects ordinary people, particularly those living on tight budgets in South Africa. 🇿🇦

## 💸 1. The Hidden Cost of Being Poor.

Researchers sometimes use the term **poverty premium** to describe situations in which people with less money can end up paying more for everyday necessities than people who can afford to buy in larger quantities or choose more economical options.

Digital technology can create similar pressures.

### 📱 When a cellphone becomes a necessity.

A modern cellphone is no longer just a device for making calls. Many people need one to apply for jobs, receive messages from employers, use banking applications, communicate with government departments, and access online services.

However, buying a smartphone is only the beginning. You may also need to pay for data, electricity to charge the device, repairs, and sometimes additional services or applications.

If your phone breaks and you cannot afford to replace it, you could lose more than your ability to make calls. You might also lose access to important services and opportunities.

### 📶 The problem with buying data in small amounts.

Someone who can afford a monthly internet package may receive better value than someone who must purchase small prepaid data bundles whenever money becomes available. The actual difference depends on the provider and package, but limited cash can restrict which offers are realistic.

And when the data runs out, internet access may disappear until more money can be found.

This creates an uncomfortable situation: you need internet access to improve your circumstances, but you need money to obtain that access.

### 💻 When older computers are left behind.

Not everyone can afford the latest computer or smartphone. Many people depend on older, refurbished, or second-hand equipment.

These devices can still be useful, but modern websites and applications sometimes demand more memory, processing power, and storage than older machines can provide.

A website that works perfectly on an expensive new computer might run slowly on an older machine. An application might refuse to install altogether.

The result can be wasted time, frustration, and sometimes the loss of an opportunity.

**🔑 The important lesson:** A person should not be considered incapable simply because their equipment is old or their internet connection is limited.

Technology should be designed to work for ordinary people, including those who cannot afford expensive equipment.

## 📊 2. What the South African Numbers Tell Us.

There is a difference between having some form of internet access and having reliable, affordable internet at home.

Statistics South Africa's **General Household Survey 2024**, released in May 2025, reported that 82.1% of households could access the internet through at least one location or method. However, only 17.4% had access through a fixed internet connection at home. Mobile internet was the most common route to getting online.

These figures tell us two things at once. Mobile technology has brought internet access within reach of many more households, but access is not the same as having a reliable home connection, enough data, a suitable device, or the skills and support needed to use online services.

The Independent Communications Authority of South Africa (ICASA), in its **State of the ICT Sector in South Africa 2026** report, also highlighted a difference between mobile and fixed broadband affordability. Its reported price baskets put a 2GB mobile broadband basket at R152 and entry-level fixed broadband at R309. These are specific comparison baskets, not the price every customer pays; actual costs depend on the service and package.

The point is not that everyone is disconnected. It is that people experience digital access in different ways, and those differences matter when essential services move online.

**Sources:** [Statistics South Africa — General Household Survey 2024](https://www.statssa.gov.za/?PPN=P0318&SCH=74038&page_id=1854) · [ICASA — State of the ICT Sector in South Africa 2026](https://www.icasa.org.za/pages/sector-forecasting)

## 🏦 3. Banking Without Being Left Behind.

Digital banking has brought real benefits to South Africa. People can transfer money, pay accounts, receive payments, and check balances without necessarily visiting a bank branch.

For someone who lives far from a branch, this can save time and transport costs.

But there are difficulties, too.

### 💳 What happens when everything moves online?

When a bank reduces its physical services or encourages customers to use an application, people are expected to have a suitable device, internet access, and the knowledge needed to use the system.

What happens if your cellphone is stolen? What if your battery dies when you need to make an urgent payment? What if your account is locked and you cannot reach a human being who can help?

These are not merely technical problems. They can become financial problems.

A person might be unable to access money, make a payment on time, or resolve an account issue quickly.

### 💰 The small charges that add up.

For someone with a comfortable income, a small transaction fee may seem insignificant.

For someone who has only enough money for food, electricity, and transport, several small charges can make a meaningful difference.

Bank fees vary between accounts and providers, so it is important to check the actual charges rather than assume that every digital transaction is free. The cost of mobile data must also be considered when an application requires an internet connection.

### 💵 Does cash still matter?

Absolutely.

Cash remains important to many South Africans, particularly where informal trading, small purchases, and limited access to banking services are part of everyday life.

A society that moves too quickly towards cashless payments risks excluding people who depend on physical money.

Digital payments should give people more choices, not take away every alternative.

**The question we should ask is simple:** Can people still buy what they need, access their own money, and obtain assistance when the technology fails?

## 🏛️ 4. Government Services: Help That Can Be Hard to Reach.

Governments increasingly use websites and digital systems to process applications, verify identities, and communicate with the public.

These systems can reduce paperwork and make services more convenient. They can also help people who would otherwise have to travel long distances to an office.

However, convenience depends on the system being accessible to the person who needs it.

### 📄 When an application becomes a mountain to climb.

Imagine applying for financial assistance and being asked to upload documents, create an online account, confirm your identity, and complete several electronic forms.

If you have a reliable internet connection and understand the process, this may be manageable.

But what if you do not know how to scan a document? What if you have no printer, your cellphone cannot open the required file, or the website keeps rejecting your application?

A process intended to make life easier can become an obstacle.

This is particularly serious when the application concerns money needed for food, housing, or other essential expenses.

### 🔐 Digital identity and access to services.

Some services require people to verify their identities electronically. This can help protect accounts and reduce fraud.

However, problems arise when access depends on a particular cellphone, a working SIM card, or an authentication method that a person cannot use.

Losing a phone or changing a number should not leave someone permanently unable to prove who they are. There must be a practical way to recover access.

### 🤖 When the computer says no.

Automated systems can process large numbers of applications, but they can also make mistakes or fail to recognise unusual circumstances.

A rejected application may be the result of missing information, an incorrect record, a technical fault, or a misunderstanding of the information supplied.

The difficulty comes when the person cannot discover why the decision was made or how to challenge it.

**People need a way to reach a real person when an automated system fails them.**

Digital services should include clear instructions, accessible support, and a fair process for correcting mistakes.

## 🚗 5. The Digital Gig Economy: Working Through an App.

One of the biggest changes brought about by the internet is the ability to find work through digital platforms.

A person can use a cellphone to accept delivery jobs, find customers, offer freelance services, or arrange transport.

In South Africa, ride-hailing, food delivery, domestic services, and other platform-based jobs have become part of the changing world of work. For people struggling to find traditional employment, these opportunities can provide a useful source of income.

But there is a catch.

### 🛠️ Who pays for the tools of the job?

In many forms of platform work, the worker must provide some or all of the equipment needed to earn money.

A delivery worker might need a smartphone, mobile data, suitable transport, fuel, and protective equipment.

A driver might also face vehicle repayments or rental costs, insurance, maintenance, and other operating expenses.

These costs do not disappear simply because the worker has not received any orders.

If the cellphone breaks, the vehicle needs repairs, or the network stops working, the worker may be unable to earn.

### 💵 Turnover is not the same as income.

Imagine a worker receives payments for several deliveries in one day.

That money is not necessarily the amount the worker takes home.

Fuel, vehicle expenses, platform charges, data, and other costs may still need to be deducted.

A busy day can therefore produce less profit than expected. A quiet day can leave the worker struggling to cover expenses.

This is why it is important to distinguish between **money received and money actually earned**.

### ⏰ Flexible work can still be difficult work.

Digital platforms often advertise the freedom to choose when to work. That flexibility can be valuable, particularly for people who have other responsibilities.

However, when income is uncertain, workers may feel pressured to remain available for long hours in the hope of earning enough. They may also face competition from other workers, changing demand, and rules they do not fully understand.

A person who is technically free to stop working may find it financially difficult to do so.

The University of Pretoria's Centre for the Future of Work describes its Fairwork South Africa project as assessing platform work against principles including fair pay, conditions, contracts, management, and worker representation. Its 2025 project page reports that researchers assessed eight platforms, interviewed 34 workers, and covered three metropolitan areas. These findings are a useful reminder that the realities of platform work should be studied through workers' experiences, not just through the convenience offered to customers.

**Flexibility is valuable, but flexibility alone does not guarantee a decent income.**

**Source:** [Fairwork South Africa — University of Pretoria](https://centrefow.com/fairwork-sa/)

## 🤖 6. When an Algorithm Becomes Your Boss.

An algorithm is simply a set of instructions a computer follows to process information or make calculations.

Algorithms can help digital platforms decide which worker receives a job, how orders are allocated, what price is displayed, or whether an account requires further checks.

These systems can operate quickly, but their decisions are not always easy for people to understand.

### 📊 Why did someone else get the job?

Imagine two delivery workers waiting for orders.

One receives several jobs while the other waits without earning anything.

There may be legitimate reasons for the difference, such as location, customer demand, availability, or the platform's allocation rules.

However, if workers cannot understand how the system operates, they may struggle to identify problems or improve their chances of receiving work.

A lack of clear explanations makes it difficult to know whether a decision was fair.

### 🚫 Locked out without warning.

Some platforms can restrict or suspend accounts when they detect suspected rule violations, unusual activity, or problems reported by customers.

Account restrictions may be necessary in certain circumstances, particularly when safety or fraud is involved.

But automated systems can make mistakes.

If a worker suddenly loses access to an account, the loss of income can be immediate. The worker may still need to pay for fuel, transport, food, or vehicle expenses.

A fair system should explain the reason for a restriction, provide a way to challenge an incorrect decision, and allow a human being to investigate where necessary.

### 🚦 When speed becomes dangerous.

Delivery workers and drivers operate in the real world, where roads are unpredictable and accidents happen.

If workers feel pressured to meet unrealistic deadlines or maintain performance ratings, they may be tempted to take unnecessary risks.

No order, rating, or delivery target is worth a human life.

Technology should support safe work rather than encourage behaviour that places workers and other road users in danger.

## 💳 7. Digital Loans: Quick Help or a Debt Trap?

A few taps on a cellphone can sometimes give someone access to credit. For a person facing an unexpected expense, that may appear to be a lifeline.

But borrowing money is not the same as earning money.

A loan must be repaid, and the repayment terms can make a tremendous difference.

### ⚠️ When borrowing makes life harder.

Consider someone who borrows money to buy food before payday.

If the person can repay the loan comfortably, and the agreement is fair, the loan may provide temporary assistance.

But what happens if the repayment leaves too little money for electricity, transport, or other essentials?

The borrower might need another loan to cover those expenses. That second loan creates another repayment obligation, which can lead to a cycle of borrowing.

The problem becomes worse when fees, interest, penalties, or collection practices place additional pressure on someone who is already struggling.

### 🧾 Not every small loan is predatory.

A small interest-free loan between people who trust each other is not automatically the same as a high-cost commercial loan.

For example, borrowing R200 or R500 without interest, with clear repayment terms and no hidden charges, can be very different from taking a loan with expensive fees and harsh penalties.

The amount borrowed alone does not tell us whether the arrangement is fair.

Before accepting a loan, ask:

- How much money will I actually receive?
- Exactly how much must I repay?
- When is repayment due?
- Are there any fees, interest charges, or penalties?
- Will I still have enough money for necessities after repayment?
- What happens if an emergency prevents me from paying on time?

If the answers are unclear, take time to understand the agreement before committing yourself.

South Africa's National Credit Act regulates the costs that credit providers may charge under covered credit agreements. The National Credit Regulator advises consumers to understand the fees and interest involved before taking credit. The details depend on the type of agreement, so do not assume that every loan has identical charges or limits.

**🔑 A useful rule for everyday life:** A loan should help solve a problem without quietly creating a bigger one.

Borrowing can sometimes be necessary, but it should never be confused with a lasting solution to insufficient income. Where credit is involved, transparency and responsible lending matter just as much as convenience.

**Sources:** [National Credit Regulator — National Credit Act](https://www.ncr.org.za/documents/pages/download_act/NATIONAL_CREDIT_ACT.pdf) · [SAnews — National Credit Regulator advice on borrowing and credit costs](https://www.sanews.gov.za/node/67084)

## 📱 8. Digital Inclusion Means More Than Owning a Phone.

Digital inclusion means that people have a realistic opportunity to use technology and benefit from it.

It is not enough to say that a service is available online. We must also ask whether ordinary people can afford it, understand it, and use it successfully.

A genuinely inclusive digital society should consider several things.

### 🌍 Affordable access.

Internet access should be reasonably affordable, with options that recognise the circumstances of people on limited incomes.

### 💻 Technology that works on older devices.

Websites and applications should be designed to work efficiently on modest computers and smartphones wherever possible.

Not every task requires elaborate applications, high-resolution graphics, or a large download.

### 📚 Education without embarrassment.

People need opportunities to learn how to use digital services safely and confidently.

Digital literacy is not a measure of intelligence. Someone can be highly capable in practical work, business, cooking, crafts, or many other fields while having limited experience with computers.

Teaching should build confidence rather than make people feel foolish for asking questions.

### 🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Human assistance when needed.

There should be a way to obtain help when a website fails, a password is lost, an application is rejected, or a person cannot complete a digital process.

Online services should not become a wall between people and the assistance they need.

### 🔒 Privacy and security.

People should understand what information they are sharing, why it is needed, and how it will be used.

Sensitive information must be protected, and systems should not collect more personal data than is reasonably necessary for their purpose.

Convenience should never mean giving up the right to reasonable privacy.

## 🇿🇦 9. What Does This Mean for South Africa?

South Africa has people who use sophisticated digital services every day, alongside others who struggle to afford data or maintain a reliable device.

These different circumstances can exist within the same neighbourhood, workplace, or family.

For one person, an online banking application is a convenient way to manage money. For another, the cost of data and the fear of making a mistake can make the same application stressful.

A young person may use the internet to learn a skill and find freelance work. Someone else may have the necessary skills but lack the equipment or connectivity needed to compete.

A digital government service may save one citizen hours of travel while leaving another unable to complete an application.

These examples show why we should be careful when declaring that technology has solved a problem.

Sometimes it has solved the problem for people who can access it, while making life more difficult for those who cannot.

### 🏘️ The importance of keeping alternatives.

South Africa needs digital services, but it also needs practical ways for people to access essential services when technology is unavailable or unsuitable.

Depending on the service, this might include telephone support, assisted applications, accessible service points, clear appeal procedures, and reasonable alternatives for people who cannot complete a process online.

The appropriate solution will differ from one service to another.

The principle, however, remains the same: **people should not be denied a fair opportunity simply because they cannot afford the technology required to access it.**

## 🧭 10. What Can We Do About It?

We do not need to reject technology to recognise its shortcomings.

Instead, we can make better choices as individuals, communities, businesses, and developers.

### 👤 As individuals.

- Compare data packages and banking fees before choosing a service.
- Keep important account recovery details safe.
- Be cautious about unfamiliar loan offers and requests for personal information.
- Calculate the real costs of platform work before deciding how profitable it is.
- Learn new digital skills at a manageable pace.

### 🏢 As businesses and service providers.

- Make websites easier to use on older devices.
- Explain charges, rules, and decisions in plain language.
- Provide accessible support when something goes wrong.
- Protect customers' personal information.
- Avoid assuming that every customer has unlimited data or the latest smartphone.

### 🏛️ As a society.

- Support practical digital education.
- Consider the needs of people living on limited incomes.
- Encourage fair treatment of platform workers.
- Preserve appropriate alternatives for accessing essential services.
- Ask whether a new digital system genuinely improves people's lives, rather than merely making administration easier.

### 💻 As developers.

Technology should be built around real people and real circumstances.

That means testing applications on modest hardware, reducing unnecessary data consumption, explaining errors clearly, and remembering that an inaccessible service is not truly accessible simply because it exists on the internet.

These are practical improvements, not impossible demands.

## ❤️ 11. The Human Being Must Come First.

Technology is a tool. Like any tool, its value depends on how it is designed, who can use it, and what it helps them accomplish.

A cellphone can connect a person to employment, education, family, and financial services. A computer can provide access to knowledge that was once difficult to obtain. An online platform can help someone turn a skill into an income.

These are genuine benefits, and we should celebrate them.

But technology can also exclude people through cost, complexity, inaccessible design, and decisions that nobody can adequately explain.

The answer is not to turn back the clock. It is to build a better digital future.

A better future recognises that people have different incomes, different abilities, different devices, and different levels of experience.

It makes room for the person with the newest smartphone and the person using an older computer. It helps the experienced user work efficiently without humiliating the beginner. It offers convenience without removing essential choices.

Most importantly, it remembers that a person is more than a customer number, a credit score, a delivery rating, or a line of information in a database.

**Technology should serve humanity, not make humanity serve technology.**

That is the standard by which we should judge our progress towards a truly inclusive digital society.

## 📝 Final Thoughts.

The digital revolution is not simply about faster computers, clever applications, or more services moving online.

It is about how these changes affect the lives of ordinary people.

For those who can afford the equipment, connectivity, and skills they need, digital technology can open extraordinary doors. For those who cannot, the same technology can become another barrier to overcome.

A fair digital society must recognise both realities.

The real measure of progress is not how much technology we introduce. It is how many people can use that technology meaningfully, safely, and affordably to improve their lives.

**The future should be digital where it helps, human where it matters, and inclusive at every step.**
