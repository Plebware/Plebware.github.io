# The Real Cost of a “Free” App.

We have become accustomed to a strange phrase in the digital world: **“It’s free.”**

Download the app. Install the app. Create an account. Press **Accept**. And away we go.

But is the application really free?

For the ordinary Plebware user, that is worth investigating.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## Free Does Not Always Mean Cost-Free.

An application may cost nothing to download while its developer makes money through advertising, subscriptions, purchases, data collection, commissions or other commercial arrangements.

That does not automatically make the application bad. It simply means that **“free” and “without cost” are not necessarily the same thing.**

For a Pleb, understanding that distinction is useful.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## What Might We Actually Be Paying With?

An ordinary smartphone user may pay for a free application in several different ways.

### 1. Attention.

Notifications, recommendations, alerts and endless scrolling can turn a five-minute visit into an hour.

Time is a resource. Once it is spent, we cannot get it back.

### 2. Data.

Applications may request access to information required for their operation. Depending on the application and its permissions, this can include location information, contacts, photographs, device information or usage information.

The better question is: **“Does this application actually need this permission?”**

### 3. Storage.

An application may grow substantially through cached files, downloaded media and stored data. On an inexpensive phone with limited storage, this matters.

### 4. Battery.

Background activity can consume battery power even when the application is not being actively used.

### 5. Money Later.

Some applications use a free entry point to introduce subscriptions, advertising or optional purchases. The important thing is knowing what we are agreeing to.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

## The Permission Test.

Before installing an application, a Pleb can ask three simple questions:

**What does this application do?**

**What information does it need to do that job?**

**Are the permissions reasonable for that purpose?**

Consider a simple calculator. If it requests access to your contacts, microphone, location and photographs, that deserves a second look.

By contrast, a navigation application requesting location access makes considerably more sense.

**The relationship between the permission and the application's purpose is what matters.**

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## Free Software Can Still Be Excellent Software.

Not every free application is harvesting personal information. Not every commercial application is exploiting its users. And not every open-source application is automatically perfect.

The PlebWare approach should therefore not be: **“Free is good. Paid is bad.”**

It should be: **“Understand what you're getting.”**

Open-source software can be particularly valuable because its source code can be inspected, studied and audited by people with the necessary skills. Commercial software can also provide excellent products and services.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## The Plebware Rule.

> **Before accepting something because it costs nothing, ask what value the other side receives.**

Sometimes the answer is perfectly reasonable. You receive a useful service. The developer receives advertising revenue. Everyone understands the arrangement.

Sometimes the answer is less obvious. That is when a little research becomes valuable.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## A Five-Minute Investigation.

Before installing an unfamiliar application, spend five minutes checking:

- Who developed it?
- What does it actually do?
- What permissions does it request?
- Does it contain advertising?
- Does it offer subscriptions or purchases?
- Does it collect personal information?
- Is there a privacy policy?
- Are there credible reviews?
- Is the application still maintained?
- Can you uninstall it easily?

Five minutes of investigation can save considerably more time later.

## The Bigger Question.

The modern Pleb is surrounded by technology: smartphones, computers, televisions, banking applications, messaging services, cloud storage, artificial intelligence and digital government services.

We increasingly depend upon systems that most of us did not build and cannot fully inspect.

That makes **digital literacy** just as important as traditional literacy.

We don't need to become programmers. We don't need to understand every line of source code. But we should understand enough to ask sensible questions.

**What am I using? What does it need? What does it receive? What am I giving in return?**

That is not paranoia. That is simply being an informed Pleb.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## Conclusion.

“Free” can be wonderful.

Free software has changed computing. Free educational resources have opened doors for millions of people. Free communication tools have connected families and communities around the world.

The problem isn't that something costs nothing. The problem is assuming that **zero price means zero cost.**

The intelligent Pleb does not reject technology. The intelligent Pleb learns how to use it wisely.

And sometimes the most valuable thing we can spend is five minutes asking:

**“What am I actually getting, and what am I actually giving?”**
