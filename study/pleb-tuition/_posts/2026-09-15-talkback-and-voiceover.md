## Welcome To Lesson 7
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

In the first six lessons, we learned what Text-to-Speech is, how to set it up, how Android, Samsung, iPhone and iPad handle speech, how to choose voices, and how to make your phone read text.

Now we are going one step further.

This lesson introduces **screen readers** — TalkBack on Android and VoiceOver on iPhone and iPad.

These tools are especially important for people who need spoken guidance while operating the entire device.

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->
## 🔑 TTS Is Not The Same As A Screen Reader
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

This is one of the most important ideas in the whole series.

**Text-to-Speech is the voice.**

It takes written words and turns them into spoken words.

**TalkBack and VoiceOver are screen readers.**

They use spoken feedback to help a person understand and operate what is happening on the screen.

A screen reader can announce things such as:

- Buttons.
- Menus.
- Links.
- Icons.
- Text fields.
- Notifications.
- The item currently selected or focused.

A simple way to remember it is:

> **TTS = The voice.**  
> **TalkBack or VoiceOver = The screen reader using the voice.**

You do **not** need a screen reader just because you want your phone to read an article aloud.

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->
## Android — Meet TalkBack
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

**TalkBack** is the screen reader built into Android devices that support it.

It provides spoken feedback and allows a person to navigate the phone using touch gestures and other controls.

The exact menus and options can differ between Android versions and manufacturers.

That is why your phone may not look exactly like somebody else's Android phone.

The important thing is to understand the job TalkBack performs.

It is there to help you **operate the whole phone**, not simply to read a paragraph.

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->
## 🔑 TalkBack Changes The Way You Touch The Phone
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

When TalkBack is active, normal touch behaviour changes.

For example, a single tap can identify or select an item, while a double tap can activate the selected item.

Some gestures used for scrolling and navigation are also different.

At first this can feel strange.

Don't panic if you switch TalkBack on and suddenly think your phone is behaving incorrectly.

It has not necessarily gone wrong.

You have entered a different way of controlling the device.

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->
## Samsung Galaxy And TalkBack
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

On a Samsung Galaxy device, TalkBack is available through the phone's Accessibility settings.

If you want to explore it, open **Settings** and search for **TalkBack**.

But remember:

If your goal is simply to make an article, message or selected piece of text speak aloud, you normally do **not** need to switch TalkBack on.

Your normal Text-to-Speech and read-aloud tools are enough for that job.

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->
## iPhone And iPad — Meet VoiceOver
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

Apple calls its screen reader **VoiceOver**.

VoiceOver is built into iPhone and iPad and provides spoken descriptions of what is on the screen.

You can find it under:

**Settings → Accessibility → VoiceOver**

Apple also provides VoiceOver practice so that users can learn its gestures.

Like TalkBack, VoiceOver is designed to help a person operate the whole device rather than simply listen to a piece of text.

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->
## 🔑 VoiceOver Also Changes How You Use The Screen
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

When VoiceOver is active, touching the screen can cause the phone or tablet to speak the item underneath your finger.

Swiping can move the focus from one item to another, and a double tap can activate the selected item.

Other gestures are also changed to support VoiceOver navigation.

Again, this can feel unusual if you have never used a screen reader before.

That is normal.

VoiceOver is changing the way you interact with the device so that spoken feedback becomes part of the navigation process.

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->
## TalkBack And VoiceOver — A Simple Comparison
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

| Feature | Android | iPhone / iPad |
|---|---|---|
| Screen reader | TalkBack | VoiceOver |
| Main purpose | Spoken device navigation | Spoken device navigation |
| Uses speech | Yes | Yes |
| Works with touch gestures | Yes | Yes |
| Designed for whole-device accessibility | Yes | Yes |
| Separate from ordinary read-aloud | Yes | Yes |

The names are different, but the basic idea is similar.

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->
## 🔑 TTS, TalkBack And VoiceOver
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

Let's put the three terms together.

**Text-to-Speech:**

The technology that converts written text into spoken words.

**TalkBack:**

Android's screen reader, which provides spoken feedback while navigating and operating the device.

**VoiceOver:**

Apple's screen reader, which provides spoken feedback while navigating and operating an iPhone or iPad.

Think of it this way:

> **TTS gives the phone a voice.**  
> **A screen reader uses that voice to explain the interface.**

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->
## When Would You Actually Need A Screen Reader?
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

A screen reader can be extremely useful when a person has difficulty seeing the screen or needs spoken guidance to navigate the device.

It can identify controls, read interface text, announce notifications and help the user move through apps and menus.

For someone who does not need full accessibility support, ordinary TTS and read-aloud features may be much simpler.

There is no prize for making your phone more complicated than it needs to be.

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->
## 🔑 Don't Turn On A Screen Reader Just To Test A Voice
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

If you are following this series because you want a pleasant voice for reading articles, books, messages or study material, stay with the TTS tools we covered in the earlier lessons.

Use **TalkBack or VoiceOver** when you specifically want screen-reader accessibility.

This distinction will save you a lot of confusion.

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->
## What If You Turned It On By Accident?
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

Don't worry.

Your phone is not necessarily broken.

You have probably activated an accessibility feature.

The exact shortcut for switching a screen reader off varies by device and software version.

If TalkBack is active on Android, you can use the device's Accessibility settings to turn it off. Some devices also provide an accessibility shortcut.

On iPhone or iPad, VoiceOver can be turned off through:

**Settings → Accessibility → VoiceOver**

If the screen behaves differently while the screen reader is active, remember that its touch and navigation gestures have changed.

## 🔑 Your Accessibility Practice Test
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

Try this simple exercise.

### Step 1

Open a familiar article or web page.

### Step 2

Listen to it using the ordinary read-aloud or TTS function you learned in Lesson 6.

### Step 3

Now, if you are comfortable doing so, explore the screen reader settings on your device.

### Step 4

Notice the difference.

Ordinary read-aloud concentrates on **reading text**.

A screen reader concentrates on **helping you understand and operate the interface**.

### Step 5

Return to your normal TTS settings when you are finished.

The purpose of this exercise is not to become a screen-reader expert in five minutes.

It is simply to understand the difference.

## The PlebWare Principle
<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

Technology should make life easier, not make it mysterious.

Text-to-Speech, TalkBack and VoiceOver are different tools with related purposes.

Once you understand the difference, you can choose the tool you actually need.

That is the PlebWare way:

**Understand the tool. Configure the tool. Then use the tool.**
