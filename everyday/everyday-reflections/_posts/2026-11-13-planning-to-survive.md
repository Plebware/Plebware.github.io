# 🧭 Surviving Everyday Life: When Making It Through the Month Becomes an Achievement.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🌍 Introduction: Survival Is Not Always What We Imagine.

When people talk about survival, we often imagine dramatic situations: natural disasters, wars, food shortages, or some extraordinary crisis.

But survival is not always dramatic.

Sometimes survival means making it through another month when the money is running out. Sometimes it means getting up after a poor night's sleep, going to work despite the heat, and trying to keep a household functioning when everything seems to demand attention at once.

Sometimes it means postponing something you desperately need because something else is more urgent.

And sometimes, survival means holding on to a dream when circumstances seem determined to push it further away.

This article is not written from a theoretical position. It comes from ordinary experiences, financial pressures, family difficulties, practical problems, and the daily decisions that many people quietly face.

The purpose is simple: to examine how we can prepare ourselves to survive difficult periods without losing sight of the fact that we are supposed to be living, too.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 💰 Part One: The Financial Domino Effect.

Financial pressure rarely arrives as one enormous event.

More often, it comes as a series of smaller disruptions. One problem leads to another, and before long, the household budget is under pressure from several directions.

A delayed payment, an unexpected expense, lost working hours, a broken appliance, or transport problems can quickly upset an otherwise carefully managed month.

The difficulty is that ordinary household expenses do not stop simply because our income has been interrupted.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🚙 When Transport Problems Become Income Problems.

Over the last two months, my employer's motor vehicle, which I affectionately call Bucky, has been in for repairs repeatedly.

The problem was not that I suddenly had to pay for an enormous vehicle repair bill. The problem was that the vehicle was not always available for work.

When the vehicle is unavailable, I cannot earn in the usual way.

After calculating the financial impact, I realised that I had lost almost **R5,000 in income**.

That is a substantial amount when you are working to keep a household going.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🤝 Sometimes Help Arrives From Unexpected Places.

During this difficult period, a friend overseas became aware of my circumstances and generously sent money.

That assistance helped cover the income losses and provided some breathing room for approximately two months.

I am genuinely grateful.

But assistance, however generous, is not necessarily a permanent solution. That money is now almost gone, and the original financial pressures have not magically disappeared.

This is an important distinction: receiving help can stabilise a situation, but it does not always remove the underlying problem.

We still need to understand what caused the difficulty and how we might manage the next interruption.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🎄 The End-of-Year Financial Squeeze.

As the year approaches its end, another pressure begins to appear.

Christmas and the festive season are heavily commercialised. Shops, advertisements, and social expectations constantly remind us that we should be buying things, travelling, entertaining, and spending money.

But not everybody has the financial resources to participate.

For some households, December is not a season of abundance. It is another month in which the electricity, food, rent, transport, and other essentials still need to be covered.

There is also an emotional side to this time of year.

When family traditions disappear because people have passed away or circumstances have changed, the festive season can become surprisingly lonely.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🧡 When Family Traditions Change.

Julian's mother, Rochelle, passed away unexpectedly in January.

She was someone who helped organise family Christmas gatherings. Her absence changes more than the number of people around a table.

It changes the traditions, the familiar arrangements, and the feeling that somebody is bringing everyone together.

This is something worth remembering when we think about survival.

Not every problem can be solved with money. Some losses change the emotional landscape of a household.

We need to make room for those realities, too.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🔑 A Practical Financial Survival Baseline.

When money becomes tight, the first task is not to create an elaborate financial plan.

It is to establish what must be protected.

Start with the essentials:

- 🏠 Accommodation and rent.
- ⚡ Electricity and basic utilities.
- 🥫 Food and household necessities.
- 🚗 Transport required to earn an income.
- 💊 Essential medication and healthcare.
- 📱 Communication needed for work and important responsibilities.
- 🐈 Necessary care for household pets.

These are the foundations.

Entertainment, upgrades, luxuries, and optional purchases may have to wait.

That does not mean they are unimportant. It means we need to distinguish between what keeps the household functioning and what can safely be postponed.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🪙 The Emergency Reserve: Even Small Amounts Matter.

An emergency reserve is useful because unexpected expenses rarely arrive at a convenient time.

Ideally, we would all have enough money to cover several months of essential expenses.

But that is not realistic for everybody.

If your income barely covers your monthly requirements, being told to save a large percentage of it can feel insulting.

Start with what is possible.

Even a small amount put aside when circumstances allow can help with a minor emergency. If nothing can be saved this month, that does not mean you have failed.

The purpose is to build a little resilience when the opportunity exists, not to make people feel guilty for being poor.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

## ☀️ Part Two: Physical Survival.

Financial pressure is only one side of the equation.

Our bodies have limits, and those limits become increasingly important when we are working long hours, sleeping poorly, and dealing with uncomfortable environmental conditions.

We cannot expect ourselves to function indefinitely without adequate rest and recovery.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🌡️ Johannesburg Heat Is Not Always Easy to Handle.

This spring has been exceptionally hot.

I am not particularly fond of high temperatures, and the heat can make an ordinary working day feel considerably longer.

Add mosquitoes, uncomfortable sleeping conditions, and the difficulty of cooling down at night, and the problem becomes more than simply feeling hot.

It affects comfort, concentration, and the ability to recover before the next working day.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 😴 Sleep Is Part of Survival.

For approximately three weeks, I have been averaging around five hours of sleep per night.

Last night's sleep tracker recorded approximately **5 hours and 43 minutes**, from 22:57 until 04:40.

Even that sleep was interrupted. There were periods when I was awake, rather than sleeping continuously.

The problem is not simply the number displayed by a sleep tracker. It is how rested we actually feel when we get up.

When poor sleep continues, concentration becomes harder, patience becomes shorter, and ordinary decisions can require considerably more effort.

We should not treat sleep as an optional luxury. It is part of maintaining the ability to work, think, and cope.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 💧 Practical Ways to Cope With Hot Weather.

We cannot control the weather, but we can make sensible adjustments.

Some practical measures include:

- 💦 Drink water regularly throughout the day.
- 🧢 Avoid unnecessary exposure to direct sunlight.
- 🌬️ Improve ventilation wherever possible.
- 🛏️ Use light bedding and comfortable sleeping clothes.
- 🚿 Take a cool shower when appropriate.
- 🦟 Use suitable mosquito protection.
- 🧊 Keep drinking water available, especially when working outdoors.

People working in hot environments should also recognise warning signs such as dizziness, confusion, severe weakness, or feeling faint.

Heat exhaustion and heatstroke are not matters of willpower. Serious symptoms require urgent medical attention.

The objective is not to become tougher than the weather. It is to avoid allowing the weather to make us ill.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🧠 Protecting Our Ability to Think.

Fatigue affects more than our physical energy.

It can influence concentration, judgement, patience, and the ability to solve problems.

When we are tired and stressed, we should avoid making unnecessary major decisions in a hurry.

Where possible:

- Write down important tasks.
- Break complicated jobs into smaller steps.
- Check figures before spending money.
- Allow yourself time to think.
- Avoid unnecessary arguments when exhausted.

Sometimes the most productive thing we can do is recognise that our minds need a rest.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

## 🧡 Part Three: Emotional Survival.

Financial problems and physical exhaustion are difficult enough.

But emotional pressure can be even harder to manage because it does not always have a clear beginning or end.

Grief, uncertainty, family difficulties, and concern for other people can occupy our thoughts even when we are trying to concentrate on ordinary responsibilities.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🕯️ Living With Loss.

Julian's mother, Rochelle, passed away unexpectedly in January.

The family has also experienced other difficult losses and circumstances.

I lost Brandon to cancer on 31 October 2024, and Rochelle passed away in January.

Every loss changes something.

Sometimes we remember the person. Sometimes we remember the conversations, the routines, or the things we expected to do together.

Grief does not follow a convenient timetable. It can surface during ordinary activities, at family gatherings, or in quiet moments when we least expect it.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 💔 When Family Problems Become Overwhelming.

There have also been serious difficulties involving members of Julian's extended family.

A family dealing with substance-related problems has faced the removal of three children by social services.

There has also been a deeply disturbing situation involving a young woman connected to the family. She was reportedly kidnapped and held for approximately a week.

After making a case, the alleged perpetrators were arrested and subsequently released on bail. She was reportedly threatened afterwards and has now been missing for approximately two months.

The family has tried checking hospitals, morgues, and police records, without finding reliable information about her whereabouts.

These are frightening circumstances.

They also demonstrate how one family's difficulties can affect the emotional wellbeing of people beyond the immediate situation.

When we care about others, we naturally want to help. But we must also recognise that some situations are beyond our ability to resolve alone.

Serious threats and missing-person situations need appropriate police and professional involvement. They should not be carried by one household in isolation.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🫂 Caring Without Carrying Everything.

There is a difference between supporting someone and accepting responsibility for every problem in their life.

We can listen. We can offer practical assistance. We can help someone find the right organisation or professional support.

But we cannot personally solve every family crisis.

Trying to carry too much can leave us emotionally exhausted and unable to deal with our own responsibilities.

Healthy boundaries are not a lack of compassion.

They help us remain available to the people who genuinely need us, without destroying our own ability to function.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🧭 Recognising Emotional Overload.

Sometimes we do not realise how much pressure we are carrying until our behaviour begins to change.

We may become irritable, withdrawn, unusually quiet, or unable to concentrate.

We may lose interest in activities that normally bring us enjoyment.

These are signals worth paying attention to.

Talking to someone trustworthy, taking a little time away from a stressful situation, or seeking professional support can make a meaningful difference.

We do not have to wait until we reach breaking point before asking for help.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

## 👟 Part Four: The Things We Keep Postponing.

One of the less obvious consequences of financial pressure is the gradual accumulation of postponed needs.

A purchase that would normally be straightforward becomes something we keep putting off.

At first, it may seem harmless.

But eventually, the postponed items begin affecting comfort, work, health, and productivity.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 👞 When New Shoes Become a Luxury.

I have not bought a new pair of shoes in more than three years.

For work and driving, I have been using safety boots.

They serve a practical purpose, but they are not necessarily comfortable for every situation. They can become hot, sweaty, and unpleasant, particularly during Johannesburg's warmer months.

Even something as basic as foot powder has become an expense I have had to postpone.

This illustrates an important point.

Survival budgeting should not only consider whether we have enough food and somewhere to sleep. It should also consider the small necessities that help us remain comfortable, healthy, and presentable.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 💻 Technology Can Be a Tool for Recovery.

Another example involves my computer equipment.

I was fortunate to receive a second-hand Lenovo laptop as a gift after my previous i7 computer, with 8 GB of RAM, developed a problem with its onboard graphics.

The replacement was useful, but it came with limitations. My earlier computer had been considerably more capable than the older dual-core Celeron machine with 4 GB of RAM that I had previously used.

The Lenovo also had limited storage space.

For somebody who writes, develops software, manages files, and works with Linux, storage is not simply a convenience. It is part of the working environment.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🔧 Making Existing Equipment Work Harder.

Fortunately, I already had several Western Digital WD Blue 500 GB SSDs available.

These were not newly purchased drives. They were equipment I already had, and one was being used in the laptop.

I eventually purchased a USB 3.0 IDE/SATA adapter, which made it possible to connect drives externally and work with them more conveniently.

The plan was to clone the smaller drive onto a 500 GB SSD and make better use of the available capacity.

The cloning process used proportional expansion, allowing the destination partitions to be adjusted to the larger drive.

There is still work to do. I want to reduce the size of the Windows partition and give Linux more room.

But before changing the partition layout, I need to create a complete backup image.

That backup is important. A storage upgrade should not become a data-loss disaster.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🛡️ Backups Are a Survival Strategy.

A computer can contain years of writing, photographs, documents, software, and personal records.

Losing that information can be devastating.

A sensible backup strategy should include:

- 💾 A complete system image before major partition changes.
- 📁 Separate copies of important personal files.
- 🔌 A reliable external storage device.
- 🔍 Occasional checks to make sure the backup can actually be restored.

A backup that has never been tested is only an assumption.

The lesson is simple: protect what you cannot easily replace.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### ✍️ The Dream That Keeps Getting Postponed.

Perhaps the most important thing I have had to postpone is my writing.

For approximately two years, my writing has taken a back seat to other responsibilities and practical problems.

I still have a goal of writing 21 science fiction books.

That is not a small ambition, especially when time, money, equipment, and energy are limited.

But writing is more than a hobby to me. It is a creative pursuit, a personal ambition, and potentially a source of supplementary income.

When we are under financial pressure, it is easy to postpone every activity that does not produce an immediate result.

The danger is that we eventually postpone the very things that might help us build a better future.

Survival should not mean abandoning every ambition.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

## 🧰 Part Five: Building a Practical Survival Plan.

A survival plan does not need to be complicated.

It needs to be realistic, flexible, and suited to the circumstances of the people who will use it.

A plan that assumes unlimited money, unlimited energy, and perfect circumstances is not a practical plan.

The best plans begin with the resources we actually have.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 1. 🏠 Establish Your Household Baseline.

Write down the expenses that keep your household functioning.

Include accommodation, electricity, food, transport, essential communication, medication, and other unavoidable commitments.

Do not begin with what you would like to spend.

Begin with what you must spend.

Once you understand the baseline, you can see how much flexibility remains.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 2. 📅 Look Ahead Before the Month Begins.

A calendar can be just as useful as a calculator.

Mark expected income dates, payment dates, recurring expenses, and any known commitments.

Look for periods when several expenses arrive close together.

If possible, prepare for those periods in advance rather than waiting until the money is already needed.

The objective is to reduce surprises, not to predict everything perfectly.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 3. 🚗 Identify Your Income Dependencies.

Ask yourself what allows you to earn your income.

It might be a vehicle, a computer, a smartphone, a particular tool, or reliable transport.

Then consider what would happen if that resource became unavailable.

Could you continue working? Is there another arrangement? Who should be contacted? What information or equipment would you need?

Understanding your dependencies makes it easier to prepare for interruptions.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 4. 🔌 Protect Essential Equipment.

Maintain the equipment that supports your work.

Keep important documents backed up. Check cables, chargers, batteries, storage devices, and other essentials.

Do not wait until something fails to discover that you have no alternative.

Where replacement is impossible, preventive maintenance becomes even more important.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 5. 🥫 Keep a Modest Household Buffer.

If circumstances allow, keep a small supply of essential household items.

This might include non-perishable food, basic cleaning supplies, toiletries, and other items that would otherwise require an immediate purchase.

There is no need to build an enormous stockpile.

The objective is simply to reduce the pressure of having to buy everything at the last minute.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 6. 🧠 Include Your Physical and Emotional Needs.

A survival plan that only considers money is incomplete.

Make room for sleep, meals, hydration, personal hygiene, and time to recover.

Also consider who you can speak to when life becomes overwhelming.

A plan should help protect the person who is doing the surviving, not merely the possessions and bills that need attention.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 7. 🎯 Keep One Longer-Term Goal Alive.

Choose something that gives you a reason to look beyond the immediate difficulties.

It might be completing a course, repairing a computer, developing a business idea, writing a book, or improving your living circumstances.

The goal does not have to be completed this month.

It simply needs to remain part of your future.

A small step taken consistently can keep an ambition alive.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

## 📆 Part Six: A Seven-Day Practical Exercise.

Sometimes the easiest way to begin is to work through one week at a time.

Here is a simple exercise that can help turn a vague intention into a practical plan.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 📝 Day One: Understand Your Current Position.

Write down the money currently available, the income you expect, and the essential expenses that still need to be paid.

Do not hide from the figures.

Knowing the situation is better than worrying about an unknown situation.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🧾 Day Two: Identify Your Priorities.

Separate essential expenses from purchases that can wait.

Be honest about the difference between something you need and something you would simply like to have.

Remember that comfort and personal wellbeing also matter. Not every non-food expense is automatically unnecessary.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🔍 Day Three: Find Your Vulnerabilities.

Ask what could disrupt your income or household routine.

Consider transport, work equipment, electricity, communication, health, and family responsibilities.

Identify the two or three problems that would cause the greatest difficulty if they happened tomorrow.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🛠️ Day Four: Take One Preventive Action.

Choose one practical improvement.

It might be checking a backup, arranging maintenance, organising important documents, or finding out who to contact if an essential service fails.

Do not try to fix everything at once.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🧡 Day Five: Check Your Support Network.

Think about the people you can approach when you need advice, assistance, or simply somebody who will listen.

Support is not always financial.

A useful conversation, a practical favour, or a reliable contact can make a difficult situation easier to manage.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🌱 Day Six: Make Room for Something Positive.

Spend a little time on an activity that gives you enjoyment or a sense of progress.

Read, write, learn, repair something, take a walk, or spend time with someone you care about.

You are more than your financial difficulties.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🧭 Day Seven: Review and Adjust.

Look back at what you discovered during the week.

What needs attention? What can wait? What did you manage to improve?

Make a short plan for the next seven days.

The exercise is not about achieving perfection. It is about developing a clearer picture of your circumstances and taking manageable steps forward.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

## 🔄 Part Seven: Adaptability Is More Important Than Perfection.

One of the biggest mistakes we can make is believing that a plan must remain unchanged.

Life does not work that way.

Income changes. Vehicles break down. People become ill. Family circumstances shift. Prices increase. Opportunities appear unexpectedly.

A useful plan must be able to adapt.

Sometimes the original goal has to be postponed. Sometimes we need to find another route. Sometimes we must accept help.

Changing the plan does not necessarily mean abandoning the objective.

It means responding intelligently to the circumstances in front of us.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### 🪜 Small Improvements Still Count.

When life becomes difficult, we often look for one major solution.

But many improvements happen through smaller decisions.

A backup completed today. A bill planned for next week. A conversation that prevents a misunderstanding. A little more rest. A postponed purchase finally prioritised.

These things may not look impressive individually.

Together, they can make a household more resilient.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

### ❤️ Survival Must Leave Room for Living.

There is a danger in becoming so focused on survival that we forget to live.

We work, pay bills, solve problems, repair equipment, manage family responsibilities, and repeat the process.

But life should not become an endless list of obligations.

We need moments of enjoyment, creativity, friendship, rest, and personal achievement.

We need something to look forward to.

Survival is important, but the purpose of surviving is to continue living.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

## 💬 Questions Worth Asking Ourselves.

Take a little time to consider these questions.

1. 💰 Do I know the minimum amount my household needs to function each month?
2. 🚗 What single disruption could affect my ability to earn an income?
3. 🛡️ Have I protected the documents, equipment, and information I cannot easily replace?
4. 😴 Am I getting enough rest to make sensible decisions?
5. 🧡 Am I carrying emotional responsibilities that I need help with?
6. 👟 What basic necessity have I postponed for too long?
7. ✍️ What personal ambition do I need to bring back into my life?
8. 🤝 Who can I approach for practical support or advice?
9. 🧭 What is one small improvement I can make this week?

These questions are not a test.

They are an invitation to look honestly at our circumstances and decide what deserves attention.

## 🌟 Final Thoughts: Keep Moving Forward.

I do not believe that surviving difficult times means having all the answers.

Sometimes it means recognising that we are under pressure and making the best decisions available to us.

Sometimes it means accepting help.

Sometimes it means postponing something we want so that we can protect something we need.

And sometimes it means refusing to abandon a dream simply because the present circumstances are difficult.

My own circumstances have reminded me that financial pressure, physical exhaustion, family difficulties, and postponed ambitions can all become connected.

A problem in one area can affect every other area.

That is why practical survival planning needs to consider the whole person and the whole household.

We cannot control everything that happens to us.

But we can learn to understand our circumstances, protect what matters, prepare where possible, and adapt when life changes direction.

**We are not simply trying to make it to the end of the month. We are trying to build a life worth continuing.**
