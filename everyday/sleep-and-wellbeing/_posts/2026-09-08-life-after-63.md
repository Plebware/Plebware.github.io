# Life After 63 — What the South African Statistics Really Tell Us.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

There is a surprisingly common misunderstanding about life expectancy.

If statistics say that the life expectancy of a South African man is around 64 years, it is tempting for a 63-year-old man to think:

> “I've almost reached the average. Perhaps I've only got a year or two left.”

That is **not** how life expectancy works.

A man who has already reached 63 has survived all the mortality risks that affected people who died during infancy, childhood, adolescence, early adulthood and middle age.

The relevant question is therefore not simply:

**“How long does a South African man live from birth?”**

It is:

**“What does the available mortality evidence tell us about someone who has already reached 63?”**

And that requires an age-specific life table.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🇿🇦 The Starting Point — South African Men.

Statistics South Africa estimated male life expectancy at birth at **63.6 years in 2024**.

That figure describes the expected lifespan of a newborn under the mortality conditions represented by the calculation.

It does **not** mean that every South African man who reaches 63 should expect to die at 64.

Life expectancy at birth incorporates mortality across the entire lifespan.

Stats SA's population modelling uses age- and sex-specific mortality rates and applies the country-specific **UN Model Life Table for South Africa** in its mortality modelling.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🧮 Why Age 63 Requires a Different Calculation.

Once somebody has reached 63, we need a **conditional survival calculation**.

In simple terms, we start with the population that has already survived to age 63 and then ask how many survive to later ages.

This is the principle behind a life table.

The Actuarial Society of South Africa maintains mortality-table resources used to estimate mortality and survival at different ages. Mortality tables form an important foundation for actuarial work involving life insurance, pensions and longevity.

Therefore, we should not take the national life expectancy at birth and simply subtract 63.

That would be statistically incorrect.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

## ⚠️ What About the 63 → 70 → 75 → 80 Percentages?

Earlier versions of this article gave approximate figures such as:

| Age reached | Earlier estimate. |
|---:|---:|
| 70 | ~78% |
| 75 | ~65% |
| 80 | ~50% |

After checking these figures against authoritative South African sources, **they should not be presented as official South African statistics**.

There is no justification for describing those exact percentages as figures directly published by Statistics South Africa.

They were estimates derived from broader mortality information rather than numbers taken directly from an identified current national male life table.

For that reason, they have been removed from this article.

That is an important correction.

## 🔍 Why This Is More Complicated Than It Looks.

South Africa has a long history of actuarial mortality tables.

The Actuarial Society maintains collections of South African mortality tables, including assured-lives, annuitant and pensioner mortality tables.

Historical South African life tables were also produced for different population groups and different periods.

Research published through the South African Actuarial Journal documents this history and explains how older South African life tables have continued to be used in some actuarial and legal calculations.

This creates an important distinction:

**A historical actuarial life table is not automatically the same thing as a current national population life table.**

And an insured population is not necessarily representative of the entire South African population.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 📊 What We Can Say With Confidence.

We can confidently say that:

**South African men have historically had lower life expectancy at birth than South African women.**

Stats SA estimated male life expectancy at birth at **63.6 years in 2024**, compared with **69.2 years for females**.

We can also say that mortality varies enormously by age.

A newborn and a 63-year-old do not face the same statistical risk.

The 63-year-old has already survived the mortality risks of the first six decades of life.

Therefore:

> **A 63-year-old man's expected remaining lifespan cannot be calculated by simply subtracting 63 from the national life expectancy at birth.**

That is the central statistical lesson.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🕰️ The Road Does Not End at the Average.

This is perhaps the most important point.

An average is not a deadline.

If a country's male life expectancy at birth is approximately 64 years, that does not create a wall at age 64.

Some men die younger.

Many live considerably longer.

Some live into their 70s.

Others reach their 80s.

Some reach their 90s.

And a small number reach 100 or beyond.

The statistical distribution matters much more than a single average.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🔑 Statistics Describe Populations — Not Destinies.

A national life table cannot tell an individual person exactly when he will die.

It does not know:

- his medical history;
- his genetics;
- his lifestyle;
- his access to healthcare;
- his socioeconomic circumstances;
- his family history;
- or the hazards he encounters during the years ahead.

Population statistics describe **patterns**.

They do not write individual destinies.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🧭 The Better Question.

Instead of asking:

**“The average South African man lives to about 64. Does that mean I've only got a year left?”**

The statistically meaningful question is:

**“Given that I have already reached 63, what does an appropriate age-specific life table say about my remaining survival?”**

That question requires the actual age-specific survival values from a suitable contemporary life table.

It should not be answered by guessing from the birth-life-expectancy figure.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## ✍️ A Final Thought.

There is something strangely comforting about getting the statistics right.

The number **63.6** may look like a finish line.

It isn't.

It is a population statistic calculated from mortality across an entire lifetime.

If you have already reached 63, you are no longer standing at the beginning of that statistical journey.

You have already travelled through six decades of it.

The road ahead still has uncertainty.

But statistics do not say:

**“Your time is up.”**

They say:

**“You have reached another stage of the journey, and the mathematics has changed.”**

And perhaps that is a better way of looking at getting older.

Not as a countdown to an average.

But as a road whose remaining distance cannot be known precisely — only estimated.
