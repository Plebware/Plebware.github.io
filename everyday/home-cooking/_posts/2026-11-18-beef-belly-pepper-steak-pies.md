# 🥩 Turning Beef Belly into Homemade Pepper Steak Pies! 🥧

Sometimes the best recipes aren't found in cookbooks. They're discovered in the kitchen, through experimentation, patience, and the determination to make good use of the ingredients available.

This particular adventure began with a gift of beef belly. The strips were thin, with a whitish layer of fat and connective tissue and a relatively thin layer of meat. Our first attempt left us with something rather difficult to chew.

Then Julian made a suggestion:

**Why don't we make pepper steak pies?**

That was the beginning of an afternoon of pressure cooking, testing, adjusting, making pastry, and finally enjoying homemade pepper steak pies.

The result? The filling was excellent. The pastry worked well too, although our first batch was a little saltier than we wanted. Here's what we learned.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🛒 Ingredients.

### For the beef filling.

The quantity below is based on an estimated 400 g packet. We didn't weigh it, so treat the amount as an approximate kitchen measure.

- Approximately 400 g beef belly.
- 1 medium onion, chopped.
- 2 cloves garlic, chopped or crushed.
- ½ beef stock cube.
- ½ teaspoon paprika.
- ½ teaspoon freshly ground black pepper, plus more to taste.
- Approximately 250 ml water, or the amount required by your pressure cooker's instructions.
- 1 teaspoon Worcestershire sauce.
- 1 tablespoon butter or margarine.
- 1–1½ tablespoons flour for thickening.
- Additional reserved cooking liquid as needed.

### For the quick shortcrust pastry.

- 2 cups ordinary flour.
- 125 g vegetable shortening.
- **¼ teaspoon salt for the improved version.** The original ½ teaspoon made our first batch a little too salty.
- 5–7 tablespoons cold water, added gradually.

If your shortening is already salted, consider reducing the salt further or leaving it out.

## 🔪 Stage 1. Understanding and preparing the beef belly.

Our beef came in strips approximately 2 cm wide and 5 mm thick. The meat itself was only about 3–5 mm thick in places, with a whitish layer behind it.

The white layer appeared to contain fat and connective tissue. The cooked strips curled, and the centre initially had a firm, gristly texture.

This was not a cut we could simply fry quickly and expect to become tender. We chose pressure cooking to soften it before making the pie filling.

Cut the strips into manageable pieces, around 1–2 cm long. There is no need to make them perfectly uniform at this stage.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🍲 Stage 2. Pressure-cooking until tender.

Place the beef in a traditional stovetop pressure cooker. Add the chopped onion, garlic, stock cube, paprika, black pepper, and water.

Follow the pressure cooker's own minimum-liquid and filling requirements. Check that the lid gasket and valves are clean and correctly fitted. Use the pressure regulator exactly as the manufacturer specifies; do not alter or dismantle its adjustment parts.

Bring the cooker to operating pressure, then reduce the heat enough to maintain the correct pressure.

### What happened in our kitchen.

Our first 15-minute session at pressure was not enough. The beef was still very chewy, with a firm, gristly centre.

A further 20 minutes improved it, but it was still not tender enough.

A third 20-minute session made a significant difference. The total cooking time was approximately **55 minutes at pressure**, excluding the time needed to reach pressure and release it.

After the final session, the meat was much easier to cut. We allowed the pressure to release safely and naturally between sessions, and never forced the lid open.

**Important:** These timings describe our particular batch and cooker, not a guaranteed time for every beef belly cut or pressure cooker. Check the meat after each session and continue only if it needs more tenderising.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## ✂️ Stage 3. The kitchen-scissors discovery.

After the final pressure-cooking session, the beef had softened considerably. The strips had curled during cooking, making them awkward to handle with a knife.

A pair of ordinary kitchen scissors proved extremely useful.

Cut the softened beef into small pieces, roughly 5–8 mm across. The scissors went through the softened meat much more easily than they had through the raw strips.

Check the pieces as you cut. Remove any hard, unpleasant gristle that remains, but keep the soft meat and tender fatty portions for flavour.

This little kitchen discovery made the preparation much easier.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🖤 Stage 4. Making the pepper steak gravy.

The reserved cooking liquid is full of flavour, so keep it. It becomes the base of the gravy.

1. Melt 1 tablespoon of butter or margarine in a separate saucepan.
2. Stir in 1–1½ tablespoons of flour.
3. Cook gently for about a minute, stirring.
4. Gradually add the reserved beef cooking liquid, stirring to prevent lumps.
5. Add the Worcestershire sauce.
6. Add freshly ground black pepper.
7. Return the chopped beef to the saucepan.
8. Simmer gently until the gravy is thick and coats the meat.

Add the cooking liquid gradually. You may not need all of it. If the gravy becomes too thick, loosen it with a little more stock. If it is too thin, simmer gently until it reduces.

Taste before adding extra salt because the stock cube and shortening may already contribute enough.

### 🧂 Freshly ground pepper makes the difference.

We used whole black peppercorns and a grinder. Grind some fairly coarsely for little bursts of pepper flavour, then adjust the seasoning towards the end.

The filling should be rich, savoury, and distinctly peppery.

**Pie-filling rule:** Keep the gravy thick. A watery filling can make the pastry soggy and difficult to handle.

Allow the filling to cool before assembling the pies.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🥧 Stage 5. Making the quick shortcrust pastry.

We wanted a straightforward homemade pastry, not puff pastry or a layered flaky pastry. This quick shortcrust version uses ordinary flour, vegetable shortening, salt, and cold water.

1. Place the flour and salt in a mixing bowl.
2. Cut the cold shortening into small pieces and add it to the flour.
3. Rub the shortening into the flour with your fingertips until the mixture resembles breadcrumbs.
4. Add cold water gradually, one tablespoon at a time.
5. Bring the mixture together into a manageable dough. Do not make it wet or sticky, and avoid kneading it excessively.
6. Cover the dough and refrigerate for 20–30 minutes.

### Our pastry adjustment.

The first batch had a good texture, but the pastry tasted a little too salty. For the next attempt, reduce the salt from ½ teaspoon to **¼ teaspoon**. If the shortening is salted, use even less or omit the added salt.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🧑‍🍳 Stage 6. Assembling the pies.

1. Lightly flour the work surface.
2. Roll the pastry to approximately 3 mm thickness.
3. Line the pie dishes or prepare individual pastry bases.
4. Spoon in the cooled pepper steak filling.
5. Cover with pastry and seal the edges with a little water.
6. Make a small opening in the top to allow steam to escape.
7. Brush the tops with beaten egg or milk for colour.

Avoid overfilling the pies. Leave enough room to seal the pastry properly.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🔥 Stage 7. Baking.

Preheat the oven to **200°C**.

Place the pies on the middle oven rack and bake for approximately **30–40 minutes**, checking as they approach the end of that time.

The pastry should be golden brown and cooked through, including underneath. If the tops brown too quickly while the pastry still needs time, cover them loosely with foil.

The beef filling has already been cooked, so this stage is about baking the pastry and heating the filling through.

Allow the pies to rest for 5–10 minutes before serving. The filling will be very hot.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

## 💡 What this kitchen experiment taught us.

- **Beef belly needs assessment.** Thin strips with connective tissue may need much longer moist cooking than ordinary steak.
- **Tenderness matters more than a fixed timer.** Our first session was insufficient; two further sessions transformed the texture.
- **Pressure-cooker safety comes first.** Follow the manufacturer's instructions for liquid, pressure regulation, and safe release.
- **Kitchen scissors can be surprisingly useful.** They made it easy to cut the softened, curled strips into small pieces.
- **Freshly ground peppercorns add character.** They gave the filling the peppery flavour we wanted.
- **Thick gravy protects the pastry.** Keep the filling rich and spoonable, not runny.
- **Season pastry carefully.** Our first batch was slightly too salty, so the revised recipe uses less salt.

## ❤️ The verdict.

The first batch was tasted, and the verdict was encouraging:

**The pepper steak filling was awesome!** The homemade pastry had a good texture, although it was a little salty. That is an easy adjustment for the next batch.

What began as an attempt to use a challenging cut of beef became a satisfying homemade pie experiment — thanks to Julian's suggestion and a little persistence in the kitchen.

Sometimes cooking isn't about having expensive ingredients or following a recipe to the letter. It's about understanding what you've got, adapting your methods, and learning as you go.

**From beef belly to pepper steak pies. That's making the most of what you have!**
