# Who Is ICASA?

If you live in South Africa and own a cellphone, use mobile data, have a fibre connection, watch television, listen to radio, use postal services, or depend on electronic communications in some other way, there is a fair chance that ICASA has something to do with the service you are using.

But what exactly is ICASA?

ICASA stands for the **Independent Communications Authority of South Africa**.

It is the country's official regulator for the electronic communications, broadcasting and postal sectors.

ICASA was established in July 2000 through the merger of two existing regulators: the South African Telecommunications Regulatory Authority, known as SATRA, and the Independent Broadcasting Authority, known as the IBA.

The postal regulator was subsequently incorporated into ICASA through the 2005 amendment process.

So ICASA was not created simply because government wanted another office full of people making rules.

It was created to bring important parts of South Africa's communications environment under an independent regulatory authority.

And there is a very important word in that description:

**Independent.**

The South African Constitution provides for institutions supporting constitutional democracy to be independent and subject only to the Constitution and the law. They are required to be impartial and to exercise their powers without fear, favour or prejudice.

They are also accountable to the National Assembly.

That means ICASA is not supposed to be a department that simply takes instructions from a politician and carries them out.

It is a regulator.

And that distinction is going to become very important as this investigation develops.

# Why Was ICASA Created?

To understand ICASA, we have to go back to the problem it was created to solve.

Communications are not an ordinary market.

You cannot simply tell a company:

> "Here is some radio spectrum. Go and build a nationwide cellular network."

Radio-frequency spectrum is a limited public resource.

Neither can every company simply dig up every road in South Africa and install its own competing telecommunications infrastructure.

Broadcasting also has a special place in a democracy.

The Constitution therefore requires legislation providing for an independent authority to regulate broadcasting in the public interest and to ensure fairness and a diversity of views broadly representing South African society.

The ICASA Act of 2000 established the institution and transferred the relevant functions of the former telecommunications and broadcasting regulators to it.

The Electronic Communications Act of 2005 subsequently became a major part of the modern regulatory framework.

The postal sector was also brought into the ICASA framework.

The idea was therefore bigger than simply protecting people from an incorrect cellphone bill.

ICASA was created to help establish an orderly communications environment in which licences are issued, scarce spectrum is managed, operators are monitored, consumers have rights, competition can exist, and communications services can be extended to people and areas that the market might otherwise neglect.

In other words, the regulator exists because the communications market has consequences far beyond the individual customer and the individual company.

It is infrastructure.

It is commerce.

It is public information.

And increasingly, it is part of everyday life.

# What Does the Law Actually Require ICASA to Do?

This is where the investigation gets interesting.

We can forget, for a moment, what ICASA says about itself.

Let us look at what the legal framework requires it to do.

ICASA's own description of its legislative mandate identifies several major responsibilities.

It must regulate the relevant sectors in the public interest.

It must issue licences.

It must make regulations.

It must impose licence conditions.

It must plan, assign, control, enforce and manage radio-frequency spectrum.

It must ensure international and regional co-operation.

It must ensure the efficient allocation of numbers.

It must ensure interoperability between networks.

It must monitor compliance with regulations and licence conditions.

It must receive and resolve complaints.

It must protect consumers from unfair business practices and poor-quality services.

It must hear and decide disputes and complaints involving licensees.

It also has responsibilities relating to competition and universal service and access.

That is a considerable amount of power.

ICASA can therefore affect who gets a licence, what conditions a licence carries, how spectrum is allocated, what operators must do, how compliance is measured and what happens when a licensee does not comply.

This is why ICASA matters to the ordinary person.

The Pleb might never walk into an ICASA office.

But the Pleb's daily life can still be affected by decisions made inside that institution.

The price of communications.

The availability of services.

The quality of a network.

The expansion of services into under-serviced areas.

The use of spectrum.

The operation of broadcasters.

The handling of complaints.

The rules governing licensed operators.

All of these sit somewhere inside the regulatory environment.

ICASA itself describes its purpose as ensuring affordable, high-quality communication services for all South Africans.

That is a very big promise.

And it gives us something measurable to investigate.

Because if the law and ICASA's own mandate are supposed to serve the public, we can eventually ask a very simple question:

**Is the public actually getting the benefit?**

# Who Is the "Public Interest" ICASA Is Supposed to Serve?

Now we arrive at the phrase that appears again and again:

**the public interest.**

But who exactly is the public?

Is it the telecommunications company?

Is it the shareholder?

Is it the broadcaster?

Is it government?

Is it the person living in Sandton with a high-speed fibre connection?

Is it the pensioner in a rural village trying to make a telephone call?

Is it the unemployed person trying to stretch a small prepaid data bundle across an entire month?

The answer cannot simply be "the industry".

And it cannot simply be "government".

The entire point of public-interest regulation is that communications services have to be considered in terms of the broader public.

ICASA says its mandate includes ensuring affordable, high-quality services for all South Africans.

It also says operators have obligations to roll out services into under-serviced areas and contribute towards universal service and access.

The Authority therefore has responsibilities that extend beyond the interests of people who already have excellent connectivity.

There is a social dimension to the mandate.

There is an economic dimension.

There is a competition dimension.

There is a consumer-protection dimension.

And there is a democratic dimension, particularly in broadcasting.

That means the "public" is not one neat group.

It includes ordinary consumers.

It includes people in under-serviced communities.

It includes businesses.

It includes broadcasters.

It includes new entrants trying to compete with established operators.

It includes people who depend upon communications services but have very little bargaining power.

And that is where the word **Pleb** becomes useful.

The Pleb is not a legal category.

It is our shorthand for the ordinary person standing at the bottom of the communications food chain.

The Pleb does not allocate spectrum.

The Pleb does not write telecommunications regulations.

The Pleb does not negotiate interconnection agreements.

The Pleb does not operate a national mobile network.

The Pleb simply wants the phone to work, the data to last, the fibre connection to perform, the bill to make sense and the service provider to treat him fairly.

If ICASA exists in the public interest, then that ordinary person must be somewhere inside the equation.

The question is whether the regulatory system delivers that benefit in practice.

We will leave that question deliberately unanswered for now.

Because an investigation should not manufacture its verdict before examining the evidence.

# Where Does ICASA Get the Money — and Who Pays?

Now we get to the question that makes this investigation particularly interesting.

**Who pays for the regulator?**

ICASA is a public institution.

It falls under Schedule 1 of the Public Finance Management Act.

It therefore operates within South Africa's public-finance framework and is accountable as a public entity.

ICASA also publishes annual reports and reports on its activities and performance.

But there is an important distinction here.

The fact that ICASA is a public institution does not mean that every rand supporting its operations simply comes from an ordinary taxpayer's income-tax payment.

ICASA operates within a broader regulatory funding system involving fees and charges associated with the industries it regulates, as well as public-sector financial arrangements.

This is exactly the area where we need to look beyond slogans.

Operators pay regulatory and licence-related fees.

Spectrum users pay spectrum-related fees.

There are administrative and other regulatory charges.

ICASA also has a statutory relationship with the Universal Service and Access arrangements, although ICASA specifically states that it does **not** administer the Universal Service and Access Fund itself. It receives monies on behalf of the Universal Service Agency of South Africa.

So the financial question is not simply:

> "Does the taxpayer pay ICASA?"

The better question is:

> **Who ultimately finances the regulatory system, and how much of that money comes from the industries being regulated, from public funds, or from other statutory sources?**

That is a question worth answering from the actual annual financial statements rather than guessing.

ICASA's latest published annual report is its **2024/2025 Annual Report**, and the Authority also publishes its annual reports and presentations to Parliament.

That financial trail is therefore available for examination.

And we are going to follow it.

Because if the companies being regulated pay regulatory fees to the regulator, that does not automatically mean that ICASA works for those companies.

But neither should we simply assume that funding arrangements have no bearing on the regulatory relationship.

**Follow the money.**

Not because following the money automatically proves wrongdoing.

But because understanding who finances an institution is part of understanding how that institution works.

# What Does ICASA Actually Regulate?

The answer is considerably broader than mobile phones.

ICASA regulates three major areas:

**Electronic communications.**

**Broadcasting.**

**Postal services.**

Within electronic communications, ICASA's responsibilities include licensing, consumer protection, numbering, regulation and compliance.

That means the telecommunications environment occupied by mobile networks, fixed communications and other electronic communications services falls within its regulatory sphere.

In broadcasting, ICASA regulates broadcasting services and licensing, manages spectrum and monitors compliance.

That includes public, commercial, community and subscription broadcasting.

And then there is the postal sector.

ICASA has responsibilities relating to the licensing and monitoring of the South African Post Office, including customer-care standards and universal-service obligations.

ICASA also manages radio-frequency spectrum.

That is one of its most important responsibilities because spectrum is a finite resource.

The regulator must plan and manage its use rather than allowing competing users simply to occupy frequencies however they please.

ICASA also collects information about the sectors it regulates and publishes State of the ICT Sector reports.

The Authority's current sector-reporting programme covers telecommunications, broadcasting and postal services, with a 2026 State of the ICT Sector report now listed on its website.

So ICASA is not simply the organisation you contact when your cellphone bill looks wrong.

It sits in the middle of an enormous communications ecosystem.

It writes rules.

It issues licences.

It manages spectrum.

It monitors compliance.

It deals with complaints.

It regulates broadcasters.

It regulates postal services.

It gathers sector information.

And it is expected to protect the public interest.

That is an extraordinary amount of influence over infrastructure that ordinary South Africans increasingly cannot live without.

# So, Is ICASA a Protector of the Pleb?

Not so fast.

That is where Captain Gemini is deliberately going to stop.

At this point we know what ICASA is.

We know why it was established.

We know what the Constitution and legislation require of it.

We know that its stated mandate includes affordability, quality, competition, consumer protection and the public interest.

We know that it has substantial regulatory powers.

We know that it is a Chapter 9 institution and therefore has constitutional independence and accountability obligations.

And we know that it is a public institution operating within South Africa's public-finance framework.

But none of those facts, by themselves, prove that ICASA is doing a good job.

Nor do they prove that it is failing.

They tell us what ICASA **is supposed to do**.

The next investigation has to examine what ICASA **actually does**.

Does a consumer who complains get meaningful redress?

Does regulation produce better prices?

Does tariff monitoring make a difference?

Does enforcement change the behaviour of large operators?

Does spectrum policy benefit ordinary users?

Does competition actually increase?

Does universal service reach the people who need it most?

And when ICASA intervenes, who ultimately benefits?

The ordinary consumer?

The regulated companies?

Government?

New competitors?

Or sometimes all of them?

Those are questions for another article.

For now, Captain Gemini has established the starting point.

ICASA was created to regulate powerful communications industries in the public interest.

The law gives it significant responsibilities.

And the Pleb has a legitimate reason to ask whether those responsibilities are being fulfilled.

**This is not a conviction.**

**It is an investigation.**

And as always:

**Follow the evidence. Don't manufacture the conclusion.**
