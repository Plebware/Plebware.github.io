# Before You Make Netflix Pay — Fix the Network.

📰 **CAPTAIN GEMINI INVESTIGATES — PART ONE**

There is an important conversation taking place in South Africa right now.

ICASA is investigating the growing influence of internet-based services such as Netflix, YouTube, WhatsApp and other so-called **Over-The-Top, or OTT, services.**

The basic question is whether these large international platforms should face additional regulation or contribute more towards the telecommunications infrastructure that carries their services.

💰 **Fair enough.**

But Captain Gemini has another question.

**Before we ask international platforms to contribute more towards South Africa's networks, shouldn't we also ask whether the networks South Africans are already paying for are delivering the service they expect?**

🔎 **That is where this investigation begins.**

## The Great OTT Debate. 📱📺

To understand what is happening, we first need to understand what "OTT" actually means.

In simple terms, an OTT service is something that uses the internet to deliver a service that once required a traditional telecommunications or broadcasting network.

Netflix doesn't need to build a television transmitter into your neighbourhood.

YouTube doesn't need its own network of cables running to your house.

WhatsApp doesn't need to build a telephone exchange every time somebody makes a call.

They use the infrastructure that already exists.

Your fibre connection or mobile connection provides the road.

Netflix, YouTube, WhatsApp and the others send their traffic down that road.

🚗 **The network provides the road.**

📦 **The internet service provides the delivery system.**

🎬 **Netflix provides the movie.**

💬 **WhatsApp provides the conversation.**

And this is where things become complicated.

## Somebody Wants Somebody Else to Pay. 💰

One of the arguments being raised internationally is known as **Fair Share**.

The argument goes something like this:

Large platforms generate enormous quantities of internet traffic.

Netflix streams movies.

YouTube streams billions of videos.

Social-media platforms move enormous quantities of photographs, video and other data.

All of that traffic travels through telecommunications infrastructure.

That infrastructure costs money to build, expand and maintain.

So network operators and others have argued that the biggest traffic-generating platforms should contribute towards those costs.

There is a reasonable question here.

But Captain Gemini has another one.

🤔 **Who is already paying for the network?**

The answer is:

**Us.**

South African consumers pay monthly subscriptions for internet connectivity.

We pay fibre companies.

We pay internet service providers.

We pay mobile operators.

We pay for the infrastructure that gives us access to the internet.

So before we decide whether Netflix or another international platform should contribute more money, perhaps consumers deserve to understand exactly what additional costs are being identified — and what any additional contribution would ultimately be used for.

## And Then There Is Mobile Data. 📱💸

But this investigation cannot stop at fibre.

There is another side to the equation that affects millions of ordinary South Africans every day.

**Mobile data.**

MTN.

Vodacom.

Telkom.

And the other mobile and telecommunications providers.

For many South Africans, mobile data isn't a luxury.

It is the internet.

It is how people communicate with their families.

It is how job seekers search for employment.

It is how small businesses communicate with customers.

It is how people access banking, government services, education, news and entertainment.

And for years, consumers have had to deal with data bundles that can expire before all the purchased data has been used.

You paid for it.

You didn't necessarily use it all.

Then the validity period ended.

⏰ **Gone.**

For somebody living on a tight budget, that can be more than an inconvenience.

It can mean having to spend money again to obtain access they believed they had already purchased.

And that raises an important question:

**Does the existing system provide the right balance between commercial sustainability and consumer value?**

That is a question worth investigating.

## ICASA Has Already Recognised the Problem. ⚖️

This isn't simply Captain Gemini raising a consumer concern.

ICASA has already taken action concerning mobile-data expiry.

New regulations provide for the rollover of certain unused data bundles, with implementation scheduled for 2027.

The issue has become significant enough that MTN and Vodacom have challenged aspects of the new rules in court.

That development is particularly interesting.

Because it tells us something important:

📱 **Data expiry is no longer just a consumer complaint.**

It has become a regulatory and legal issue.

And that brings us back to the bigger question.

If regulators believe consumers need greater protection when purchased data expires, then what does that tell us about the relationship between telecommunications companies, regulators and the people paying the bills?

If the connection is suffering from latency, I want to understand what is causing it.

And if the provider cannot immediately identify the cause, I want to know what steps are being taken to investigate it.

## Speed Isn't Everything. ⚡🐌

There is another misconception we need to clear up.

A fast internet connection isn't necessarily a good internet connection.

You can have impressive download speeds and still experience:

- High latency.
- Jitter.
- Packet loss.
- Poor routing.
- Congestion.
- Unstable connections.
- Slow responses from particular services.

Imagine buying a Ferrari and being told:

> "Look how fast this car can go!"

Then discovering that the roads you actually use are congested, poorly maintained or simply not suited to that advertised top speed.

🏎️💨 **Top speed doesn't tell the whole story.**

The same principle applies to the internet.

Captain Gemini wants to investigate **the actual experience of the customer**, not simply the number printed on an advertising brochure.

## The Poor Are Not the Problem. 🇿🇦

There is another part of this conversation that cannot be ignored.

South Africa has a very large population of people living under severe economic pressure.

Official unemployment was **32.7% in the first quarter of 2026**.

Latest poverty statistics also show that approximately **23 million South Africans were living below the lower-bound poverty line**.

For somebody living on a very limited income, every rand matters.

Every gigabyte matters.

Every unexpected charge matters.

Every expired bundle matters.

And this is where Captain Gemini wants to challenge the direction of the conversation.

When ordinary South Africans struggle to afford connectivity, employment and basic necessities, we should be careful about treating the consumer as though the consumer is responsible for the economic circumstances surrounding them.

A person who cannot find a job did not create unemployment.

A person earning a very low income did not design the telecommunications system.

A family struggling to pay for data did not establish the regulatory framework.

A consumer who loses unused data did not write the terms and conditions governing the bundle.

So perhaps the investigation needs to travel further **up the chain of responsibility.**

⬆️ From the consumer.

⬆️ To the service provider.

⬆️ To the regulator.

⬆️ To the policymakers.

⬆️ And ultimately to the institutions responsible for the country's infrastructure and economic environment.

The question is not simply:

**"Why can't the consumer afford more?"**

Perhaps we should also ask:

**"How can the system work better for the people who can afford the least?"**

## Who Is Actually Being Asked to Pay? 💰🔍

This is where the Captain Gemini investigation becomes uncomfortable.

The international technology companies are enormous.

Netflix is enormous.

Google and YouTube are enormous.

Meta and WhatsApp are enormous.

They operate at a scale that is difficult for an ordinary consumer to comprehend.

So it is entirely legitimate to ask whether they should contribute fairly to the infrastructure on which their businesses depend.

But fairness has to work **both ways.**

If an international company is asked to contribute more money towards South Africa's telecommunications infrastructure, then South Africans deserve to know:

**Where does that money go?**

**Who controls it?**

**What infrastructure does it fund?**

**How is the spending measured?**

**How does the consumer benefit?**

And perhaps the most important question:

> **What mechanisms will ensure that additional contributions result in meaningful investment and improved infrastructure rather than simply becoming another cost within the system?**

Because adding another source of funding does not, by itself, guarantee a better network.

## The Real Problem May Be Accountability. ⚖️

This is not an argument that telecommunications companies are dishonest.

It isn't an argument that regulators are incompetent.

And it certainly isn't an argument that every problem in South Africa can be attributed to one organisation.

Reality is more complicated.

But it is reasonable to ask whether everyone involved in the system is being held to an appropriate standard of accountability.

When your bill is due, you are expected to pay.

When your data expires, you lose access.

When your contract is breached, there can be consequences.

So what happens when the service provider gets something wrong?

What happens when a consumer believes they have been overcharged?

What happens when data expires before it has been used?

What happens when a network consistently suffers poor performance?

What happens when a complaint isn't properly answered?

And what mechanisms exist to make sure infrastructure investment ultimately benefits the people using the network?

These are not anti-business questions.

They are **consumer accountability questions.**
