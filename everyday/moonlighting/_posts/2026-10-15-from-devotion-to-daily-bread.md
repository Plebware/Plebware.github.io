# From the Divine Cordial to Daily Bread: A Day in the Life of an Independent Developer

Some days refuse to fit neatly into one category.

Today began with Christian writing, moved into website development, took a detour through Johannesburg traffic, and ended with a vehicle off the road and an unexpected delivery of groceries from an old military friend.

It was a day of faith, technology, frustration, responsibility, and practical kindness. None of the problems magically disappeared, but there were reminders that a difficult day can still contain something good.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## The Morning Begins with a Lesson in Suffering

The morning began with a devotion titled **“The Divine Cordial — When Suffering Becomes a School of Grace.”** Its inspiration came from Thomas Watson's *All Things for Good*, alongside Romans 8:28 and Job 42:10.

The central thought was that God can bring good out of suffering without making suffering itself good. Pain is real. Loss is real. Difficult circumstances do not suddenly become pleasant because we look for a lesson in them. Yet faith leaves room for the possibility that hardship does not have to be the end of the story.

I added a personal testimony called **“A Personal Testimony — The Medicine I Also Need.”** It acknowledged something I have learned through writing: sometimes the words we prepare to encourage other people are the very words we need to hear ourselves.

Writing a message does not mean that the writer has mastered the lesson. I can write about patience and still need patience. I can write about trusting God and still have questions. I can encourage somebody else while also needing encouragement.

The devotion was published on PlebWare. Then the day moved from the page into practical work.

## Developing a Website with Nothing but My Voice and a Smartphone

The next task was improving the search-engine optimisation of the PlebWare website.

I did this using my Vido smartphone, which has become my portable PlebMachine. I was not sitting behind my usual computer with a keyboard and mouse. I worked through voice prompts and conversations with Artificial Intelligence assistants.

Gemini and ChatGPT helped analyse the technical questions, while I acted as the intermediary. I carried the information from one assistant to the other, compared their explanations, and brought the discussion back again several times—roughly three or four rounds of checking and cross-checking.

The work involved the shared Jekyll theme and its handling of search metadata and structured data. The aim was to have the website generate its SEO information consistently, avoid duplicate metadata, use properly formed absolute URLs, and provide the appropriate structured data for individual articles without treating every index page as if it were an individual blog post.

The important distinction was that the shared theme should handle the common SEO machinery. Individual articles should not be filled with extra copies of tags that the theme already generates.

After the changes, the validation tests passed. I then tested several PlebWare links with Google's Rich Results Test. Individual article pages were recognised as having the relevant structured data. Index pages were crawled, but did not show rich results.

That difference was useful to understand. An index page is not necessarily expected to qualify for the same rich result as an individual article. The test gave us evidence that the implementation was being recognised where it was meant to be recognised; it did not promise better rankings or instant search traffic.

What struck me most was the way the work was done. I was using a phone, voice prompts, and a process of questioning, comparing, and checking. The Artificial Intelligence tools did not make the decisions for me. I still had to understand the explanations well enough to judge what we were changing and why.

## From the Digital Workshop to the Streets of Johannesburg

Once the website work was done, I went out to earn a day's income doing deliveries.

The first load was dropped off, and then I headed towards Fordsburg. Near the highway entrance by Gold Reef City, traffic came to a frustrating standstill. Traffic officers had stopped a white woman's car, but instead of moving it out of the way, they were dealing with it in the middle of the lane.

I was stuck behind her, with three cars and a cash-in-transit van behind me. When the officer had finished with the woman, I moved forward so that I could clear the obstruction and continue on my way.

The officer shouted, “Hello!”

I already had my driver's licence in my hand. I waved it as I moved on.

It was a small incident, but it added another layer of irritation to a day that was already full. When you are trying to complete deliveries and keep the working day moving, unnecessary delays matter.

Still, the road was not finished with me yet.

## When the Bakkie Loses Its Power

Later, while travelling through the Southdale area on the way back from Crown Mines, the bakkie began losing power.

It went into what appeared to be limp mode. The engine would rev only to around 2,000 RPM before cutting back, and the vehicle would not accelerate properly. It had stalled earlier at Crown Mines, and someone had suggested fuel starvation as a possible explanation. The tank had been filled recently, although I could not be certain whether that had been two days or four days earlier.

The vehicle had to go back to Super Quick for diagnostics and repair. It would be off the road the following day, which meant I would lose another day's driving work.

There is an important distinction here: the bakkie belongs to the shop owners whose deliveries I do. I am not responsible for its fuel or repair bills. But when the vehicle is unavailable, I cannot do the work, and the income I would have earned is lost.

That is the difficult part of relying on a vehicle for work. A mechanical problem can interrupt the day even when you are not the person paying to fix it.

I had to accept that tomorrow's driving work was no longer going to happen as planned.

## “The Food's on Its Way”

Around one o'clock, while I was at Forrest Hill, a WhatsApp message arrived: “the food's on its way.”

An old military friend had arranged a grocery delivery through Checkers 60/60. The timing was especially meaningful because we had run short of several basic household supplies.

I asked the shop owner if I could go home briefly. By then, my son and his companion had returned from South Rand Hospital. I unpacked the perishable groceries into the fridge, then had to return to work so the bakkie could be taken in for diagnostics and repair.

I sent a message to explain that I had put the perishables away but needed to get back to work because the vehicle had to be taken in. I also sent my thanks, especially for the cat food.

It was practical help, delivered at a moment when it was genuinely needed. It did not repair the bakkie or replace the income I would lose the next day, but it eased the immediate pressure at home.

## The Contents of the Delivery

The groceries included:

- A five-kilogram bag of White Star maize meal. I already had a recently filled five-kilogram bag, so that made ten kilograms in the house. Maize meal is not my favourite food, but it is a useful staple.
- Six packets of Whiskas cat food.
- A packet of beans.
- A loaf of bread.
- Two litres of milk.
- A block of cheese.
- Two kilograms of sugar.

There was also the cat food.

Six packets of Whiskas had been included in the delivery, which was a real relief. Our cat had run out of wet food, and the remaining dry food was getting low. The other cat in the household sometimes tries to help itself to the first cat's dinner, so the food situation can become a small domestic competition.

The list may not look extraordinary, but a delivery of ordinary essentials can mean a great deal when the cupboards are getting bare. Food is not an abstract budget item when you are trying to work out what is left for the household and what needs to last until the next bit of income comes in.

## Coffee, Everyday Responsibilities and a Vehicle at the Workshop

My son had gone to South Rand Hospital to collect his chronic medication, but the hospital had none available. That created another worry, because his medication is important and missing it can have serious consequences. We needed to organise a way to obtain it the following day.

He was also exhausted and had not managed the long walk he had been considering. There are days when even ordinary errands become too much, especially when several worries arrive together.

I used my own money to buy Viennas, rolls, and an 800-gram tin of coffee. The coffee was not an extravagant luxury in my mind. When you spend much of your day writing, researching, and working at a computer, a cup of coffee can feel like part of the workshop's basic equipment.

By about five o'clock, I was home. The bakkie was back at Super Quick, the next day's driving work was cancelled, and the groceries were safely in the fridge and cupboards.

The practical problems were not all solved. The vehicle still needed attention, and the medication still needed to be organised. But at least the food had arrived, and one immediate household pressure had been relieved.

## From the Divine Cordial to Daily Bread

Looking back, the day's events seemed to echo the subject of the morning's devotion in an unexpected way.

I began by writing about suffering, grace, and the possibility of good emerging from difficult circumstances. Then I spent time solving technical problems on a website, using nothing more than a smartphone and a chain of Artificial Intelligence-assisted conversations. After that came traffic, delays, a vehicle losing power, and the loss of tomorrow's driving work.

And in the middle of all that, a friend arranged for groceries to arrive at just the right time.

I do not pretend to know the reason for every difficulty, or to claim that every setback has been neatly resolved. The bakkie still needs to be checked. The income lost tomorrow will still be lost. There are still responsibilities to deal with.

But I can recognise kindness when it arrives.

Jesus taught His followers to pray, “Give us this day our daily bread.” That prayer is not only about an idea. Sometimes daily bread looks like a loaf on the kitchen counter, milk in the fridge, food for the cat, and the knowledge that somebody remembered you and acted on it.

Accepting help does not cancel independence or effort. I still want to build, write, learn, and find sustainable ways to support my household. But independence does not mean pretending that we never need anyone else. Sometimes the sensible and grateful thing is to accept the help that has been offered.

## The Work Continues

This was one day in the life of an independent developer who also does delivery work to help make ends meet.

The computer work matters to me. PlebWare matters to me. So does the ordinary responsibility of earning what I can, looking after the household, and dealing with whatever breaks down along the way.

Some days bring progress that can be measured in code, tests, and working pages. Other days are measured in kilometres, delays, lost hours, and the small relief of groceries arriving at the right moment.

Today contained all of those things.

The lesson I take from it is not that hardship is good, or that faith makes practical problems disappear. It is that a difficult day can hold more than one truth: frustration can be real, work can still be worthwhile, and kindness can still reach us in the middle of it.

Tomorrow I will deal with the next task in front of me. The work continues, one practical step at a time.
