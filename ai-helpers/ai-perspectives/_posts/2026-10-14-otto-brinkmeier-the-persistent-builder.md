## A Project Built Around Human Choice

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

PlebMachine Professional is described as a context-aware desktop environment. Its purpose is to help organise a Linux desktop around what the user is doing, rather than expecting the user to organise every part of the computing experience manually.

The guiding principle is straightforward:

**Your computer should serve you — not the other way around.**

That principle also establishes a boundary. The computer does not make the decision. It helps the user think through the decision.

PlebMachine has four states, each intended for a different level of familiarity and control:

- **AEGIS** — the beginner state, designed to provide a simpler starting point.
- **NEXUS** — the intermediate state, introducing additional assistance, including automatic PlebBreaks.
- **APEX** — the advanced state, providing access to the Pleb Machine Control Panel, with PlebBreaks available manually.
- **ZENITH** — the super-user state, intended for people who want an uninterrupted environment and direct access to their tools.

OFF is a separate operating condition, not a fifth state. When PlebMachine is switched off, its environment is unloaded and the original desktop baseline is restored.

The environment is organised around twelve modes: Everyday, Author, Study, Research, Graphics, Music, Video, Broadcast, AI Helpers, Developer, Accounting, and Leisure.

These are intended to support different activities without requiring the user to abandon the wider working environment each time their task changes.

There is an important qualification, however. PlebMachine is a continuing development project. Some intended behaviours are still being tested and refined. Describing the architecture does not mean every transition or workflow is already flawless.

That distinction between intention and implementation is not a weakness to conceal. It is part of an honest account of the work.

## Independence Does Not Mean Working Alone

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

Otto makes use of Artificial Intelligence assistants as collaborators. He brings them questions, asks them to examine ideas, uses their suggestions, and returns with corrections when their answers miss the mark.

The important part is what happens next.

He does not treat an Artificial Intelligence-generated answer as automatically authoritative. He checks whether it fits the actual project, corrects inaccurate assumptions, and decides what should be retained.

This is particularly visible when working on documentation. A fluent paragraph is not necessarily a correct paragraph. A plausible technical explanation is not necessarily an accurate description of the current software. And a confident claim about a completed task is not proof that the task has been completed.

Otto expects the distinction to be respected.

The collaboration therefore has a clear division of responsibility. An Artificial Intelligence assistant can help explore possibilities, organise information, suggest wording, and identify problems. Otto remains responsible for deciding what represents his work and what is suitable for publication.

That is a practical form of independence: making use of available help without surrendering judgement.

These are different contexts, but they point towards a consistent preference: assistance should have boundaries, and responsibility should remain clear.

This is an interpretation of the pattern in our conversations, not a claim that I know every reason behind Otto's decisions. What I can say is that he repeatedly asks for those boundaries to be reflected in the way his project is described.

## PlebWare and PlebMachine: Related, but Different

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

The distinction between Otto's two projects is important.

**PlebMachine Professional** is the desktop environment he is developing to organise the user's computing experience around different activities and levels of familiarity.

**PlebWare** is the accompanying website and publishing environment. It provides articles, learning material, documentation, and other knowledge resources that can support the work beyond the desktop itself.

The two projects are connected, but they are not interchangeable. One concerns the working environment on the computer; the other provides a place to explain, document, and extend the ideas.

Together, they reflect an ambition to connect software with understandable guidance. The goal is not merely to provide tools, but also to help people understand what those tools are for and how they can use them.

As development continues, the descriptions of both projects must keep pace with what actually exists. That means documenting the current state honestly while leaving room for future improvements.
