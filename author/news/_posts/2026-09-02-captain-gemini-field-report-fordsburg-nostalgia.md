# Captain Gemini in Fordsburg — A Journey Through Old Memories.

**Fordsburg, Johannesburg — 2 September 2026.**

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## The Second Field Report of the Day.

Captain Gemini's correspondent, Mr. Brinkmeier, is once again out on the road.

This time, the vehicle is sitting in Fordsburg, outside Kolaba Butcher, established in 1961.

Two full quarters of beef and two boxes of beef liver have been loaded.

The next stop is just across the plaza, where a few small supplies will be delivered. After that, it is back to Forest Hill to unload.

The day's work continues.

But sitting here in Fordsburg has triggered something unexpected.

Nostalgia.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## When Fordsburg Was Part of the Working Life.

Fordsburg has changed.

Mr. Brinkmeier used to work here, around the corner at the railway depot.

Back then, the facility was known as the Electrical Train Sheds, Braamfontein.

The railway organisation went through several identities during his working life. When he was there, it was known as Metro Rail. Before that came Transnet, and before that, when his railway career began, it was the South African Railways.

Eleven years were spent working in that depot.

Now, sitting here decades later, the buildings and businesses may have changed, but the memories haven't disappeared.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## Chips, Russians and Bomber Jack.

There were lunchtime escapes in those days.

Mr. Brinkmeier would come out of the depot and buy chips and "Russians" — which, he recalls with a grin, weren't Russians at all.

Then there was Bomber Jack at Akawela's Café.

The game became a small lunchtime competition.

There was another railway worker who liked taking the high score, and Otto occasionally found himself "stealing time" from the working day just to put another score on the clock.

It was harmless mischief, but it made the lunch breaks memorable.

Sometimes the smallest pieces of a working life become the memories that survive longest.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## The Plaza and the First Car Sound System.

Mr. Brinkmeier also remembers walking across to the plaza, where he knew a man named Mohammed who sold car sound equipment.

It was from Mohammed's stock that Otto bought his very first car sound system.

It was a Pioneer system with a front-loading CD player and two big Pioneer 12-inch subwoofers.

That was only the beginning.

Later, Otto built another system into his Toyota Corolla.

This one used a Tornado amplifier and another pair of big subwoofers — 10-inch units this time.

The installation was a proper piece of backyard engineering.

The speakers were mounted into a fibreglass enclosure built into the spare-wheel area. A wooden frame and false floor were added, raising the boot floor of the Toyota hatchback.

It was the 12-valve model with the slightly square body, but when the hatch was opened, the installation wasn't obvious.

The entire floor was covered with a cushy carpet.

The speakers were hidden.

The amplifiers were underneath.

And the result was reportedly capable of producing around **130 decibels** inside the vehicle.

Not bad for a home-built car audio system.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## The Sound of Another Era.

There is something wonderfully nostalgic about remembering a car not because of its make or model, but because of the sound system hidden inside it.

The Toyota became more than transport.

It became a rolling sound experiment.

Fibreglass.

Wood.

False flooring.

Hidden amplifiers.

Big subwoofers.

And enough bass to make the boot feel like a small concert hall.

Those were the days when building your own car sound system was part engineering project, part art form and part madness.

Mr. Brinkmeier remembers them fondly.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## Waiting for the Buyer.

And now, back in the present, Captain Gemini's correspondent is sitting in the vehicle once again.

The beef is loaded.

The liver is loaded.

The next delivery is waiting.

The final run back to Forest Hill is still ahead.

For the moment, however, there is nothing to do but wait for the buyer to return so the day's work can continue.

And perhaps that is what makes this particular field report different.

The road has stopped for a moment, and memory has caught up with the present.

From the railway sheds to the butcher's shop.

From lunchtime chips to Bomber Jack.

From railway work to building 130-decibel car audio systems.

A working life leaves traces in strange places.

Sometimes you only notice them when you sit still for a few minutes.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## Captain Gemini's Field Assessment.

Fordsburg is not the same place Mr. Brinkmeier remembers from his railway days.

The railway organisation has changed names.

The businesses have changed.

The streets have changed.

The young railway worker who once walked out for chips and played Bomber Jack is now sitting in a vehicle waiting for another delivery to be completed.

But the memories remain remarkably clear.

Captain Gemini's assessment is simple:

**A place can change completely and still remain part of your history.**

Today, Fordsburg isn't just another delivery point.

For a few minutes, it became a doorway into the past.

And sometimes, while waiting for the next job, that's exactly where a field reporter needs to be.
