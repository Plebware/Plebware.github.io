# 🁢 The Dominoes Might Have Fallen—Beyond Excuses!

Five small stories about ordinary people, life's falling dominoes, and the faith that helps us keep going.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

## 🎣 The Fisherman.

Othello was just another ordinary man who made his living beside the river of life. Then, one day, forty-four years ago, he met a man unlike any he had ever known. Before long, Othello and many others from his village had left their old lives behind to follow this remarkable man. It is said that, even today, they are still catching fish of another sort, and that they have discovered a kind of sustenance that hunger can never take away.

Othello had recently written about the domino effect and how it affects us both financially and emotionally. Lately, however, he had felt his own dominoes beginning to fall again.

Their work vehicle had been in and out of workshops. First, there had been a gearbox noise and a temporary fix. Later came problems with the computer box following the installation of an alarm. The frustration seemed never-ending.

Over the past two to three months, poor Othello had lost many days of work: four days here, another four days there, and a few more scattered in between. It all added up. It was hard to believe that one problem with a vehicle could set so many other dominoes tumbling, placing such a strain on their finances.

Yesterday, Othello tried to keep himself occupied and take his mind off his troubles. He worked on the website and even wrote an article about the domino effect. He also climbed a ladder and repaired the carport gutter. Technically, that was his landlord's responsibility, but doing it himself was quicker and, in its own way, therapeutic. He even taped up a crack in the carport roof to stop a leak, although he knew that a proper repair would still be needed later.

There was plenty of other work to occupy him. He was busy completing his PlebMachine project and had started writing its manual. On top of everything else, he and his household had been dealing with intermittent network problems. Othello had taken the modem down, cleaned away the dust, checked the cables, and reconnected everything. Unfortunately, the problems persisted.

Sometimes, it seemed that every time he managed to straighten one domino, another was already preparing to fall.

There was another element to this domino effect, too.

Boy Mist, Othello's friend, partner, and fellow fisherman, had been combining his finances with Othello's for more than twenty years. Together, they had learned to navigate the ups and downs of life, sharing what they had and helping each other through difficult times.

This month, when Boy Mist went to the hospital to collect his chronic medication, he discovered that the hospital had no stock available. There was little choice but to purchase the medication privately at their local pharmacy, costing them a few hundred rand out of their own pockets.

For people already living from hand to mouth, an unexpected medical expense can be enough to knock another domino over. It was not a fortune to some, perhaps, but it was money they had not planned to spend, and every rand already had a purpose.

Still, Othello had learned that keeping busy could help him cope with the pressure. For now, that meant fixing what he could, writing whenever he could, and occasionally playing a game to clear his head. He could not repair everything at once, but there was no point in surrendering to the frustration either.

Despite the setbacks, Othello Verrocchio and Boy Mist still managed to put a meal on the table each day. Their circumstances were far from ideal, and neither man was pretending otherwise. Yet, even in hardship, they continued to follow the Man who had called His first disciples to become fishers of men.

Their fishing had changed, but their calling had not lost its meaning.

They were still learning to trust God when the nets came back nearly empty, when the bills arrived at the wrong time, and when one unexpected problem threatened to undo the progress they had made.

Perhaps this was another lesson in the art of fishing: a fisherman cannot command the river, the weather, or the fish. He can prepare his nets, work diligently, and persevere, but there are things beyond his control.

Faith does not promise that every day will bring a full catch, nor does following Christ guarantee a life free from financial hardship. Rather, faith gives us a reason to keep going when circumstances threaten to overwhelm us.

And so Othello and Boy Mist continue their journey. They may not always know where the next catch will come from, but they know that their hope rests in Someone greater than their circumstances.

Their earthly nets may sometimes be nearly empty, but they have discovered that the treasure of following Christ cannot be measured in fish, money, or possessions.

For they are still catching fish of another sort, and they hold on to a hope that the falling dominoes cannot take away.

## 🏺 The Potter’s Apprentice.

Young Fred had never realised that becoming a potter's apprentice would be such a long process. He had fancied himself creating beautiful clay pottery, yet, after forty-eight years of following his Master, he had spent much of his apprenticeship learning how to repair broken vessels.

To become a master potter, Fred was discovering, required more than learning a craft. He had to do not only the will of his Lord, but also the will of his Lord's Father. And that meant allowing himself to be shaped before he could hope to help shape others.

One of Fred's most painful lessons concerned the brokenness that can arise from careless words.

A few years earlier, a situation involving gossip had left a lasting impression on him. A well-meaning brother had been chatting with the pastor when Fred's name came up in conversation. During that discussion, the brother falsely accused Fred of using money from the church's Isaiah 58 assistance fund to buy cigarettes.

The accusation was untrue.

Sadly, the conversation had consequences beyond the two men involved. The church eldership subsequently changed its policy regarding financial assistance to struggling congregants. Instead of providing cash, the church began issuing Shoprite grocery vouchers to help people obtain food.

The irony of the situation was difficult to overlook. Fred had always regarded blessings, whatever their form, as gifts from God. When he received assistance, he was grateful, and he used it to provide food and meet his needs. He was not using the church's assistance to fund his smoking habit. He worked to support his own personal habits at a local business near his home, while the help he received was used for necessities.

The greatest irony was that the brother who had spread the accusation was himself a smoker and a recipient of the same Isaiah 58 assistance. He, too, eventually received Shoprite vouchers rather than cash.

One careless conversation had helped bring about a change that affected more than one person. Whatever the brother's intentions may have been, the consequences extended beyond Fred and reached other people who depended on the church's generosity.

It was a reminder that words can travel much farther than the person speaking them ever imagines. An assumption can become an accusation, an accusation can influence a decision, and a decision can affect people who had nothing to do with the original conversation.

In the years that followed, Fred received kind assistance from friends and family, including groceries passed on through a non-profit connection. Some of that support had changed more recently. Nevertheless, Fred remained grateful for the help he had received over the years. He understood that people gave according to their own convictions and that circumstances could change.

He did not wish to turn his disappointment into an accusation against those who had helped him. He knew that many had acted with good intentions, and he appreciated the kindness they had shown him.

There was, however, another part of Fred's story that deserved to be told honestly.

Yes, Fred still smoked. He knew that smoking was harmful to his health, and he was still working towards giving it up. But smoking was not the only habit from which he had needed deliverance.

Fred had once drunk excessively. In 1999, with the help of his Potter, he gave up alcohol. In 2014, he also gave up marijuana.

Those victories mattered. They were reminders that change was possible, even when the journey was long and the work remained unfinished.

Fred had not conquered every weakness, but neither had every weakness conquered him. He continued to trust that God could work in his life, helping him grow in the areas where he still struggled. He wanted to leave smoking behind in God's timing and through God's strength, rather than pretending that the struggle did not exist.

Being an apprentice of the Potter did not mean that Fred had become a finished vessel. It meant that he was still being shaped.

And sometimes, when support changes or old disappointments resurface, the experience can feel painfully familiar. It is a kind of déjà vu that reaches beyond memory and touches the soul. Fred knew that feeling, and he knew how easily disappointment could become bitterness if he allowed it to take root.

Yet this story is not told to criticise the church, its leadership, or the brother whose careless words helped set these events in motion. Those who helped Fred did so with the intentions and convictions they held, and Fred remains thankful for the kindness he received.

Rather, the story is a reminder that our words carry responsibility. We may speak without thinking, repeat something we have not verified, or make an assumption about another person's conduct. We may even believe that we are acting in someone's best interests. But once those words leave our mouths, we cannot always control where they travel or what damage they cause.

A broken vessel can sometimes be repaired, but the work requires care, patience, and a willingness to acknowledge the damage.

Fred is still an apprentice. He is still learning to resist old habits, forgive disappointments, and trust the Potter with the unfinished parts of his life.

Perhaps that is why the Potter has kept him busy repairing broken vessels. Before Fred can understand the work of restoration in the lives of others, he must continue to experience the Potter's patient work in his own.

The clay is not yet finished, and neither is the apprentice.

And as long as Fred remains willing to be shaped, there is still hope for what the Potter can make of him.

## 🪖 The Combat Soldier.

William was drafted into the King’s army and taught to fight with weapons he had never imagined using. Long before he was drafted to serve his own country, however, another calling had already begun to take shape.

Words and truth were key elements in his arsenal. Discipline and patience were also required in large quantities. William had learned to fight with real weapons, too, during the two years of continuous military service he was required by law to complete. Today, he is one of the least-known combatants in a war that has lasted longer than any kingdom, empire, or earthly nation. Yet he continues to play a vital role in the longest-standing war of all time: the battle between truth and falsehood, good and evil, and the light of God against the darkness of this world.

William soon discovered that this was not the sort of war in which medals were handed out for every victory. He never even received the medal due to him for the service he had given his motherland and country. After he was decommissioned from the army, the physical patrols ended, but the war did not. The battlefield had shifted from the African savanna and desert to battles in the spiritual realm. Some struggles took place in the privacy of his own thoughts; others found him standing in the middle of a disagreement, trying to speak the truth without wounding someone unnecessarily. There were moments when remaining silent required more courage than speaking, and others when keeping quiet would have been the easier way out.

He had traded his automatic rifle for a keyboard, which he came to realise could be mightier than the rifle, the pen, and even the ancient quill. 💻✍️

His King and original Commander-in-Chief had given him an unusual set of weapons. He had the Word of God, prayer, patience, and the ability to encourage those who had grown weary. He was also learning that truth could be spoken without cruelty, and that defending the faith did not mean treating every person who disagreed with him as an enemy.

Of course, William did not always get it right. Sometimes pride crept into his words, impatience took the place of understanding, and he discovered that the soldier who wished to correct everybody else first had to examine his own conduct. His training was far from complete.

There were days when William wondered whether his small efforts made any difference. The world seemed full of arguments, accusations, broken promises, and people who preferred a convenient lie to an uncomfortable truth. What could one ordinary soldier possibly accomplish against such overwhelming odds?

Then he remembered the instructions given to soldiers in the King's service. The apostle Paul had written about putting on the whole armour of God, standing firm in the truth, and taking up the shield of faith. William began to understand that his task was not to win every argument, but to remain faithful to the One who had called him.

He was not fighting against flesh and blood. People were not his ultimate enemy, even when their words or actions caused him pain. His responsibility was to resist evil, expose falsehood where necessary, encourage the discouraged, and remain faithful when the cost of doing so became uncomfortable.

William also learned that a soldier could not spend every moment charging into battle. Sometimes he needed to rest, pray, listen, and allow his Commander to teach him something new. Courage without wisdom could become recklessness, while truth without love could become a weapon used to wound rather than heal.

And so William continues to serve. He may never be celebrated in the newspapers, even though he holds a diploma in journalism, nor will his name appear in the history books alongside the great generals of the world. But every time he chooses honesty over deception, forgiveness over bitterness, and faithfulness over convenience, another small victory is won.

William is an ordinary man serving an extraordinary King. His battlefield may be his home, his workplace, his church, or even the quiet corridors of his own mind. Yet he knows that the war is not over, and that some of his hardest battles may still lie ahead.

But he has learned one important lesson: a soldier of Christ does not measure victory merely by how many enemies he defeats, but by how faithfully he follows his King.

And when the final trumpet sounds, William hopes to hear the words that every faithful soldier longs to hear: “Well done, good and faithful servant.”

## 📚 The Teacher’s Apprentices.

A great teacher goes around recruiting new students and turning them into good students.

This teacher always teaches his students the same curriculum, a curriculum taught by the greatest Teacher of them all. And that, my dear friend, is why the Teacher’s Apprentices are so numerous in our world today.

The first thing a new apprentice discovers is that becoming a student of this Teacher is not simply a matter of sitting in a classroom and memorising a collection of lessons. His teachings must be lived. A person can know the right words, quote the correct passages, and explain the lesson to everyone else, yet still struggle to practise what has been taught.

The Teacher is patient with His apprentices. He knows that some learn quickly while others need a little more time. Some arrive with broken hearts, others with proud minds, and a few come believing that they already know everything worth knowing. Each has something to learn, and each must be prepared to have their understanding challenged.

His curriculum is unlike any other. He teaches His students to love their neighbours, forgive those who have wronged them, care for the poor, show mercy to the overlooked, and seek the truth even when it is uncomfortable. He reminds them that greatness is not measured by how many people serve them, but by their willingness to serve others.

The apprentices soon discover that the lessons do not end when the classroom door closes. Indeed, that is where some of the most difficult examinations begin.

It is easy to speak about forgiveness when nobody has offended you. It is easy to preach generosity when your cupboards are full. It is easy to tell another person to remain patient when you are not the one waiting for an answer to prayer. But when disappointment arrives, money becomes scarce, or someone speaks carelessly about you, the Teacher's lessons suddenly become very practical.

Some apprentices pass these examinations with flying colours. Others stumble, make mistakes, and return to their Teacher seeking guidance. The wonderful thing is that failure need not mean the end of their education. A repentant heart can learn from a mistake, make amends where possible, and continue growing in wisdom.

The Teacher also has a remarkable way of sending His students back into the world. Once they have learned something of value, they are encouraged to share it with others. They do not have to become famous preachers or respected scholars. A kind word, a helping hand, an honest answer, or a quiet act of forgiveness may become the very lesson another person needs.

And so the apprentices become teachers themselves, not because they have replaced their Master, but because they have learned to pass on what He has taught them. They invite others to become students, and those students, in turn, encourage still more people to follow the same curriculum.

Of course, the apprentices must remember that they remain students too. The moment one of them believes he has graduated beyond the need for correction, he may discover that pride has become his newest and most troublesome lesson.

The greatest Teacher is Jesus Christ. His original disciples learned from Him, carried His message into the world, and taught others to observe His commands. That work continues through generations of believers who seek to live according to His Word.

The classroom may be a church, a family home, a workplace, a hospital ward, or a conversation between two people who happen to meet at the right moment. The lesson may be delivered through Scripture, through the example of a faithful Christian, or through an experience that teaches us something about our own hearts.

The Teacher's Apprentices are everywhere, but they are not called to compete with one another for the title of greatest student. They are called to grow in grace, to love one another, and to make disciples of all nations.

And perhaps that is the most important examination of all: not how much we know about the Teacher, but how faithfully we live according to what He has taught us.

For the greatest Teacher has never stopped calling new students. There is still room in His classroom, and there are still lessons to be learned.

## 🐑 The Sheep Wrangler.

It was with reluctance that Brother Cody accepted the calling of sheep wrangler from his Master.

It was not that Cody was ungrateful for the opportunity. It was just that he had never envisioned himself as a wrangler of sheep. He would have preferred to be given cattle or horses to wrangle instead.

Cody had always imagined that a worthwhile calling would involve something a little more impressive. Cattle were strong animals, and horses had a certain dignity about them. Sheep, on the other hand, seemed to spend an extraordinary amount of time wandering off, getting themselves into trouble, and requiring somebody to bring them back.

Nevertheless, the Master had made His decision, and Cody was learning that a calling was not always a matter of personal preference. Sometimes it was a matter of trusting the One who knew more about the work than the person being called to do it.

His first lessons were not particularly glamorous. Sheep needed feeding, water, protection, and patient attention. They could become frightened by things that seemed insignificant to a human observer, and a small opening in a fence was sometimes all the invitation they needed to wander into danger.

Cody soon discovered that keeping sheep was not simply about telling them where to go. He had to pay attention to where they were, notice when one was missing, and recognise when an animal was struggling. The healthy sheep might manage on their own for a while, but the injured and frightened ones needed particular care.

There were times when Cody grew impatient. Why could the sheep not simply follow the path? Why did the same problems keep appearing? Why did he have to repeat instructions that he had already given a dozen times?

Then he remembered that his Master had never promised him a flock of perfect sheep.

Cody himself was still learning. He, too, had wandered from the right path on occasion, needed correction, and depended on the patience of his Master. How could he demand perfection from those entrusted to his care when he was still being shaped by the same hands?

The work also taught him something about responsibility. The sheep did not belong to Cody. They belonged to the Master. Cody had been entrusted with their care, not given permission to treat them as his possessions.

That distinction mattered.

A wrangler who cared only about keeping the flock obedient might become more concerned with his own authority than with the welfare of the animals. But a faithful shepherd had to be willing to seek out the one that was missing, tend to the one that was wounded, and protect the flock from dangers that it could not recognise for itself.

Cody began to understand why Jesus had spoken so often about sheep and shepherds. In the Gospel of John, Jesus describes Himself as the Good Shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep. After His resurrection, He instructed Peter to feed His lambs and tend His sheep.

The responsibility was not merely to gather a crowd or make people follow instructions. It was to care for them, teach them, encourage them, and point them towards the One who was their true Shepherd.

This calling could be tiring. Sometimes those whom Cody tried to help misunderstood his intentions. At other times, he had to accept that he could offer guidance without controlling another person's decisions. He could point out the safe path, but he could not force every sheep to take it.

There were also days when Cody questioned whether he was suited to the task at all. He had his own shortcomings, his own struggles, and his own lessons to learn. How could someone still being taught by the Master be expected to help others?

Then he began to see that perhaps this was precisely the point. The Master had not called Cody because he was already a perfect shepherd. He had called him to serve, to learn, and to depend on the Master's wisdom rather than his own.

Cody still occasionally wished he had been given cattle or horses. He had not suddenly become a man who never grew tired or impatient. But he was beginning to understand that faithfulness was not measured by how attractive a calling appeared to be, nor by how closely it matched one's personal ambitions.

It was measured by what one did with the responsibility God had entrusted to them.

And so Brother Cody continues to wrangle his sheep, learning as he goes. He tries to be patient with the wandering, gentle with the wounded, and watchful when danger approaches. He knows that he will make mistakes, but he also knows where to turn when he needs wisdom.

For Cody is not the owner of the flock, nor is he the Good Shepherd. He is simply a servant entrusted with a little portion of the Master's work.

And perhaps that is enough.

After all, the sheep belong to the Master, the calling belongs to the Master, and the glory belongs to the Master.

Cody's task is simply to remain faithful.
