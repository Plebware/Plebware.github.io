# 📚 Laconic, Laconic, Laconic.

*How one English assignment diminished a young student's confidence — and how he found his way back to language.*

Some memories remain with us not because they lasted a long time, but because of what they changed. A single classroom incident can leave a mark that follows us for years, shaping the way we approach a subject, a teacher, or even a part of ourselves.

This is the story of a word I discovered through reading, a school assignment I believed I had completed honestly, and an accusation that changed my relationship with English.

It is also the story of how, many years later, I found my way back.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 📚 The Library That Shaped My World.

My father had built up an extensive library at home. Books were part of the furniture of my childhood, and they opened doors to places and subjects far beyond our own front door.

There were Reader’s Digest yearbooks from 1967 to 1973, a blue science encyclopaedia that I believe may have been from the Grolier collection, and sets of *Popular Mechanics*. There were books about nature and crafts, condensed books, and novels by Agatha Christie. There was always something to investigate, something to read, or something new to learn.

One especially memorable collection was devoted to international cooking. I cannot remember how many volumes there were, but each hardcover cookbook had its own spiral-bound recipe book. I remember titles and subjects such as American, German and Austrian cooking, and one called *Scandinavian Cooking*. I believe there were Spanish and Italian volumes as well. There was even a book about wines. 🍲🌍

The collection was a reminder that books could introduce you to other countries and their traditions without your ever leaving home.

I read widely. During my compulsory military service, which began in January 1983, I enjoyed Louis L’Amour and J. T. Edson Westerns, as well as science fiction. Reading was not merely a school activity; it was a pleasure and a way of exploring the world.

That home library helped shape my curiosity. It also taught me to look things up. If I encountered a word I did not understand, I could reach for a dictionary and investigate it.

I was a reader long before I became a confident writer.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 📝 The Word That Started It All.

At school, we were given an English assignment to write a book review. I wanted to do it properly, so I read, thought about the author's style, and searched for words that expressed what I wanted to say.

I noticed that the author wrote in a concise way. I wanted to describe that quality accurately, rather than settling for a vague word such as “short” or “brief”.

So I consulted a dictionary.

That was where I came across the word *laconic*.

I liked it. It was precise, expressive, and just right for what I wanted to communicate. I understood it as describing speech or writing that uses few words, often in a crisp and direct manner.

I used the word in my assignment because I believed it was the right word. I had not copied somebody else's review. I had found the word through my own reading and dictionary research, and I was pleased to have discovered a more exact way of expressing an idea.

For a young student, that was part of the excitement of language: finding a word that fitted the thought.

I handed in the assignment believing I had done my work honestly.

I had no idea that one word was about to become attached to a memory that would stay with me for decades.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 💔 An Accusation I Never Forgot.

My English teacher accused me of plagiarism. She did not believe that I could have written the review myself, and the assignment was failed.

I remember protesting that the work was mine. I had read the book, written the review, and looked up the word because I wanted to use language accurately.

My recollection is that the teacher opened a dictionary there and then to look up *laconic* herself.

What hurt was not simply the mark. It was the feeling that my effort and honesty had been dismissed before they were properly considered. I had tried to improve my writing, and the very word I had chosen to make my work more accurate became the reason I was not believed.

I was a young pupil in Grade 10, then known as Standard Eight. I did not have the confidence or the vocabulary to explain the experience as I might explain it today. I only knew that I had tried, that I believed I had done nothing wrong, and that I had been punished for something I had taken pride in.

A teacher's doubt can carry considerable weight when you are still learning to trust your own abilities. An accusation can become more than a judgement about one piece of work; it can feel like a judgement about who you are and what you are capable of doing.

I have never forgotten that moment.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 📉 When Enthusiasm Became Disengagement.

After that experience, my marks in English fell. My enthusiasm faded, and the subject became something to get through rather than something to enjoy. I moved from the higher grade to the standard grade.

I cannot say that one classroom incident explains every later academic decision. Life is rarely that simple. But I remember how that experience changed my attitude. The confidence I had brought to the assignment was replaced by a reluctance to put myself forward.

When a pupil feels that trying harder may only lead to embarrassment or disbelief, it becomes tempting to stop trying so hard.

That is one of the quiet costs of discouragement. It may not be visible in a single report card. It can appear in the questions a student no longer asks, the words they no longer risk using, and the opportunities they decide are not worth pursuing.

I still loved reading, but formal English had become tangled up with an unpleasant memory. The subject that might have helped me express myself had instead become a source of frustration.

The word *laconic* remained with me, though. In a strange way, it became a reminder of both my curiosity and the moment that had discouraged it.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🔑 Twenty Years Later — Reclaiming My Words.

Roughly twenty years later, I began finding my way back to language.

Over the years, my interests and experiences had continued to develop. During the COVID-19 period, I enrolled in language-usage courses and completed a journalism course. I was once again studying how words work, how ideas can be communicated clearly, and how a sentence can be improved without losing the thought behind it.

This time, I was choosing to learn for myself.

I could return to grammar, usage, and writing without being the schoolboy who had once been disbelieved over a dictionary word. I could ask questions, make mistakes, and improve through practice. Language was no longer simply a subject in which I had once received a disappointing mark. It was a set of tools I could keep learning to use.

There is something deeply satisfying about reclaiming an interest that you once associated with hurt. It does not erase the old memory, but it changes the story around it. The incident becomes one part of a much longer journey, rather than the final word.

I had not stopped being curious. My curiosity had simply needed time and room to find its voice again.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🌱 What the Experience Taught Me.

Looking back, I think about what might have happened if my teacher had approached the assignment differently.

She could have asked me where I had encountered the word. She could have invited me to explain what it meant and why I had chosen it. She could have used the moment to encourage a pupil who was trying to expand his vocabulary.

A teacher is entitled to question work that raises concerns. Academic honesty matters. But an unfamiliar word, by itself, is not proof of plagiarism. A question and a conversation can establish far more than an assumption.

The same principle applies beyond the classroom. When someone does something unexpected or demonstrates knowledge we did not anticipate, we should be careful not to confuse surprise with evidence. Asking how a person arrived at an answer is often more useful than immediately deciding they could not have arrived there themselves.

Education should make room for curiosity. It should teach students how to find information, evaluate it, and use it honestly. A dictionary is not evidence of cheating; it is one of the tools through which learning happens.

I wish that experience had been handled differently. I cannot change what happened, but I can share what I learned from it.

If you teach, mentor, or encourage someone who is still finding their feet, remember that your response may stay with them longer than you realise. Correct the work when correction is needed. Ask questions when you have doubts. Give people an opportunity to explain themselves.

**Correct the work, but do not crush the curiosity behind it.**

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## ✍️ Laconic, Laconic, Laconic — A Final Reflection.

The word *laconic* means much the same to me today as it did when I first found it in a dictionary. What has changed is the story I attach to it.

For years, the memory reminded me of being disbelieved at a time when I was trying to do well. Now it also reminds me that a painful experience need not have the final say in our lives.

I returned to language. I studied usage. I completed a journalism course. I kept reading, learning, and writing.

I cannot recover the confidence of that particular schoolboy in precisely the form it once existed. But I can honour his curiosity by continuing to learn, and I can remember that he was not wrong to look for a word that expressed his meaning.

Perhaps that is the quiet victory: not proving the old accusation wrong to the person who made it, but refusing to let the accusation define the rest of the journey.

If a word has ever opened a door for you, do not be ashamed of walking through it. Keep reading. Keep asking. Keep learning how to say what you mean.

And never allow one person's disbelief to convince you that your desire to learn was a mistake. 📚
