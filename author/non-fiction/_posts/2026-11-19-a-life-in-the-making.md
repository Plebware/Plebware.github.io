# A Life in the Making — My Journey Through the Years.

This is not the story of a straight road from childhood to success. My life has taken a few turns, some expected and some that I could never have planned. I have worked with my hands, learned technical trades, built businesses, cared for family, made mistakes, started again, and continued looking for ways to create something useful.

Some years are clear in my memory. Others are less certain, and there are still chapters I will add as they come back to me. I would rather leave a gap than fill it with a story that is not true.

What follows is a record of the experiences that helped make me the person I am today.

## 👶 1963–1969 — Where My Story Began.

I was born on **18 January 1963** at **South Rand Hospital**, Johannesburg, South Africa.

My mother was **Maria Johanna Brinkmeier**. The earliest years of my life are naturally difficult to reconstruct from memory. Much of what I know about that period comes from family records, documents and stories rather than a clear recollection of every event.

One date I can establish is **12 January 1969**. That was my first day in Grade 1 at **Park Junior School**, on Hay Street in Turffontein.

School opened the door to a wider world beyond home. I cannot pretend to remember every classroom, teacher or childhood conversation, but the dates and places give me a starting point from which to rebuild the story.

## 🧩 A Boy Who Loved Building Things.

From an early age, I was fascinated by how things fitted together and how they worked.

Meccano gave me metal parts, nuts, bolts, gears and beams with which to build cranes, bridges and machines. LEGO opened up another world of construction, while a chemistry set introduced me to experiments and reactions.

I was not simply passing time with toys. I was discovering the pleasure of taking separate pieces and turning them into something that had a purpose.

That interest followed me into electronics. At around sixteen, I used a 150-in-1 electronics kit to build a wired listening device. The circuit itself was only part of the challenge; I also tried to conceal the wiring around the house. My mother eventually discovered what I had done, told my father, and he confiscated the kit.

Looking back, the episode taught me more than a lesson in electronics. I had thought about whether I could build the device, but not properly about the privacy of the people around me. Technical ability also needs judgement.

The tools would change as I grew older, but the instinct to investigate, build and solve problems stayed with me.

## 🔧 The Workshop, the Garden and the Practical Lessons.

My interest in making things was not limited to electronics. At around fourteen, I remember the idea of having a workshop of my own. My father began building a corrugated-iron lean-to beside his brick workshop. The project was abandoned after an upsetting incident involving a neighbour's remark. It remained one of those childhood memories that took many years to look at differently.

As an adult, I continued finding practical solutions to everyday problems. At about twenty-four, I designed and installed a four-zone garden irrigation system. It involved steel pipework, fabricated brackets, PVC distribution lines, lever-operated valves and pop-up sprinklers. Working around my railway employment, I completed the project over three weekends.

I also built furniture and workspaces, including an entertainment unit and an electronics test station. These projects demanded measuring, planning, fabrication and patience.

They were all expressions of the same approach: identify a problem, understand what is needed, and build a solution that works.

## 🚆 The Railway Years — Learning a Trade.

A major chapter of my working life began in the South African railway environment. I worked through the organisational changes from **South African Railways**, to **South African Transport Services**, then **Transnet**, and eventually **Metro Rail**.

My work was connected with electrical motive power and railway equipment. It was a demanding environment where precision mattered. Heavy machinery does not forgive careless work, and a fault must be understood before it can be corrected.

On **7 June 1988**, I completed my Electrical Fitter trade test, specialising in Electric Motive Power. My certificate was issued on **28 June 1988**.

My professional development also included a St John Ambulance First Aid certificate issued on **7 February 1986**, valid until 7 February 1989; specialised railway electrical training dated **18 September 1992**; and the Inter-Lead TeamBuilder course, completed between **21 and 25 January 1996**.

Those qualifications represent more than paperwork. They record years of practical experience, safety awareness, technical learning and the development of the ability to work with other people.

The railway taught me that systems must work in real conditions. It also taught me that mistakes have consequences, and that responsibility is part of being a tradesman.

## 💻 The Computer Dream.

While I was still working at the railway, I became increasingly interested in computers. Mainstream machines were expensive, so I bought what I could afford: a **Spectravideo** home computer. I cannot remember the exact model, but I remember discovering that it was not merely a games machine. It could be programmed, and experimenting with BASIC opened a new door for me.

At the time, I had ambitions that were considerably bigger than my experience. I dreamed of building something significant on the internet, although I did not yet know exactly what that would become.

The important thing is that the interest was already there. I wanted to use computers to create things and make technology useful. The name PlebWare and the architecture of PlebMachine were still far in the future, but the underlying curiosity had begun.

## ❤️ 1999–2004 — A Family Responsibility.

In **1999**, I resigned from the railway so that I could look after my mother.

That decision changed the direction of my working life. I was leaving an environment I had known for years and taking on a personal responsibility that mattered more than the career I was stepping away from.

My mother, **Maria Johanna Brinkmeier**, passed away in **January 2004**. I do not want to guess at the exact day; I am recording the month and year that I can confidently remember.

Her death marked another turning point. The responsibility that had shaped my decision to leave the railway had come to an end, and I had to work out what the next chapter of my life would look like.

## 🖥️ 0tronix Developments — Turning an Interest into a Business.

In **2003**, I founded **0tronix Developments**. The name was deliberately written with a zero at the beginning and pronounced “Zerotronix”.

It began as a media and information-technology business. The work included website development and maintenance, graphic design, technical documentation, educational material, multimedia and practical computer services. I also helped small businesses with administrative systems and documentation.

I had the ambition to build a sustainable business around the skills I was developing. The experience taught me that being able to do the work and being able to make a business profitable are two different challenges.

0tronix eventually had to close because it was not financially sustainable. That did not make the experience worthless. It gave me practical experience in dealing with clients, producing work, managing responsibilities and learning what a small business requires.

## 🛠️ Shopfitting, Handyman Work and British American Tobacco.

Another important part of my working life involved practical site work. I did work with handyman teams and became involved in shopfitting through a subcontracting arrangement with a shopfitting company.

As part of that work, I installed and repaired cigarette kiosks and unitary for **British American Tobacco South Africa (BATSA)**. This work continued for more than five years.

It was a different environment from the railway and from running a computer business. It required practical problem-solving, working on site and getting installations and repairs done properly.

I want to record the relationship accurately: I was working as a subcontractor to the shopfitting company, not claiming that I was directly employed by BATSA.

The different jobs may appear unrelated, but they all relied on the same practical foundation: understand the task, work with the materials and tools available, and find a solution that does the job.

## 🌐 2006 Onwards — The Beginning of PlebWare.

In **2006**, I met **Julian Boyd de Villiers**, known to me as Juelz. He became a friend, creative partner and an important part of my life. We began working together on ideas, online platforms and creative projects.

Juelz brought audio-production skills into the collaboration, and we also worked with **Martin de Walt** during the early development of the PlebWare identity.

The name **PlebWare** grew out of that period. It represented an idea that technology and knowledge should not belong only to large organisations or specialists. Ordinary people should be able to learn, create, repair and make practical use of technology.

PlebWare became the formal identity of the project in **2010**. Its roots, however, reach back to the earlier 0tronix work and the collaboration that began in 2006.

Over the years, our work has included websites, online platforms, music production, documentation and experiments with different technologies. We have had successes, disappointments and plenty of reasons to start again.

The relationship and the project have both evolved. They are part of my personal history as well as the history of the work.

## 🐧 From Computers to Linux and a New Kind of Workshop.

As my interest in computers developed, Linux became an important part of my working environment. It offered the opportunity to understand the system, adapt it and keep learning rather than treating the computer as a sealed appliance.

That way of thinking connected naturally with my earlier experience as an electrical fitter and practical problem-solver. Whether I was tracing a fault in equipment, building a website or configuring a desktop, I wanted to understand how the pieces fitted together.

PlebWare gradually grew beyond its original ideas. It became a place to publish articles, preserve knowledge, create educational material and explore practical technology.

PlebMachine is part of that continuing journey. Its purpose is to help people organise their computing while keeping the human operator in control. The principle behind it is simple:

> The computer does not make the decision. It helps you think through the decision.

## ✍️ The Writer, the Builder and the Man Still Learning.

My life has never fitted neatly into one profession. I have been an electrical fitter, railway worker, small-business owner, shopfitter, computer enthusiast, website builder and writer.

I have also written under different creative identities for different kinds of work. My fiction and creative writing use the name **Othello Cody Verrocchio**. My Christian writing has its own identity, while my personal and technical work is generally published under my own name.

Writing has become another way of examining the experiences behind the work. It allows me to preserve memories, document lessons and share what I have learned. Some stories are straightforward; others take time to understand.

There are still years I have not filled in here. There are people, jobs, projects and events that deserve their own place in the story. I will add those chapters as I recover the details and confirm the dates.

## 💙 Looking Back — The Journey Is Still Underway.

When I look back, I see a life made up of many different kinds of work. Childhood experiments led to practical projects. Practical projects led to a trade. The railway provided years of technical experience. Family responsibility changed my direction. Business and subcontract work brought new challenges. Computers opened another world, and PlebWare gave some of those interests a shared identity.

Not every plan succeeded. Some projects ended. Some decisions were difficult. There were periods when I had to begin again.

But the thread running through the years is still there: curiosity, practical problem-solving, creativity and the desire to make something useful.

I am not writing this as a claim that I have finished the journey or achieved everything I once imagined. I am writing it because the journey itself deserves to be recorded.

**The tools have changed. The work has changed. But the builder is still here.**
