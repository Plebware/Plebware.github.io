# 🌳 When the Garden Died: Twenty-One Years of Memory, Loss, and Forgiveness.

*Some wounds are measured in years. Others are measured in the things that can never grow back.*

There are memories that time softens, and others that remain pressed into the mind like dried leaves between the pages of an old book. They may lose their colour, but their shape remains. Every so often, something opens that book again, and suddenly we find ourselves standing in the past, looking at what was lost.

Twenty-one years have passed since that autumn evening, yet I can still remember the silence that greeted me. It was heavier than usual, a sullen void hanging over a yard that had, just hours before, breathed with life, memory, and green majesty.

I was left laconic, struck utterly mute by the sheer scale of the desolation before me.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🏡 A Home That Had Lost Its Green Crown.

Just a year after my mother's passing, I had sought comfort in the familiar routines of home, living alongside my fiancée—the neighbour's daughter, whose family history was inextricably bound to our own.

Home was supposed to be a refuge. The garden was part of that refuge, a living extension of the house, a place where roots took hold, seasons unfolded, and ordinary days acquired a quiet beauty of their own.

That morning, I had left for work as I ordinarily did. I had no reason to imagine that I would return to a landscape stripped of its former glory.

But when I stepped onto the property after a long day's work, the landscape had undergone a ruthless, incomprehensible amputation.

The twin conifers that once stood like stoic sentinels at our gates—one soaring an imperial eighteen feet into the sky—were gone. The four-foot rose bushes guarding the entrance had been sheared away.

It was as though someone had taken a knife to the face of my home and removed its expression.

Along the left flank of the backyard, where an orchard had offered a quiet bounty, lay a wasteland of stumps: the fig tree, the trio of delicate peach trees, and, most painfully, the crown jewel—the lemon tree that bore fruit so vibrant and sweet no market could ever replicate it.

Further in, the central pear tree and the apple tree in the main garden had been reduced to memory.

Where there had been leaves, branches, shade, and the promise of another season, there was now emptiness.

The garden had not merely been trimmed. It had been erased.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 💰 The Cost of What Cannot Simply Be Replaced.

There was another reckoning beneath the shock, one that perhaps takes longer to understand when we are young and life has already handed us more than our share of burdens.

**What had all that growth cost me?**

Not merely in money, but in time, effort, patience, and the small sacrifices through which a home gradually becomes one's own.

Trees do not appear overnight. They have to be selected, bought, planted, watered, protected, and nurtured. Their roots must find their way into the earth. Their branches must survive the seasons. A young tree requires years before it becomes a mature presence in a garden.

The money spent on a tree is only the beginning of its price.

There is the water used to keep it alive, the labour invested in the soil, the time spent tending it, and the hope that one day it will reward that care with shade, blossoms, or fruit.

The lemon tree was a particularly painful example. Its fruit possessed a sweetness that had become part of my experience of home. No trip to the market could purchase the history behind it. A replacement might provide lemons one day, but it could not give me back the years already invested in that tree.

And what of the conifers? One had climbed eighteen feet towards the sky. How many seasons had passed while it grew to that height? How much patience had gone into watching it become part of the entrance to the property?

A mature tree is a living investment, but it is not an investment that can be restored by simply withdrawing money from a bank account.

Even if I could have afforded to buy replacements, I could not have purchased an instant return to the garden I had left that morning.

There was no receipt that could calculate the full loss. There was no invoice that could itemise the years. There was no reasonable sum of money that could return the familiar landscape to the place where it had stood.

And so the financial questions remain with the emotional ones, even after all these years.

What had I spent? What would it cost to replace everything? How long would I have to wait for the new trees to grow? And why should I have to bear the expense of rebuilding something that I had not chosen to destroy?

Those questions are not necessarily signs of greed or bitterness. Sometimes they are the mind's attempt to make sense of an injury that has no sensible explanation.

The ledger of a destroyed garden contains more than money.

It contains years.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🔥 The Fury Beneath the Silence.

A white-hot, blinding rage surged through me, an incandescent fury born of violation.

How could anyone commit such an act of wanton destruction?

I could understand pruning a tree, removing a diseased branch, or changing a garden through a considered decision. Gardens evolve. Homeowners make changes. Living things sometimes have to be removed for sound reasons.

But this was different.

I had gone to work that morning and returned to discover that a substantial part of my world had been taken from me without my consent.

The anger was not simply about timber, leaves, or fruit. It was about the sudden discovery that something I had cared for could be destroyed while I was absent, leaving me with no opportunity to intervene.

There was a profound helplessness in that realisation.

The trees could not explain what had happened. The stumps could not tell me why. Their silence seemed almost accusatory, as though the garden itself had been forced to witness an injustice and was now waiting for me to understand it.

I remember the questions that must have tumbled through my mind.

Why would anyone do this?

What had the trees done to deserve it?

What had I done to deserve coming home to such devastation?

And perhaps the most painful question of all: **How could someone so closely connected to my life have allowed this to happen?**

I cannot reconstruct every thought I had that evening. Memory is not a recording machine. It preserves some details in extraordinary clarity while allowing others to fade.

But I know the devastation was real, and I know that the anger was real.

Twenty-one years later, I can still understand the man who stood in that garden and struggled to comprehend what he was seeing.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🛋️ A Quiet Grace Beneath the Carport.

Sitting later on the couch beneath the carport, grappling with the shock, a quiet grace interrupted the storm.

I believe God gently reminded me that I was holding onto an unyielding bitterness.

The anger had its reasons. The destruction had been real. Forgiveness did not require me to pretend otherwise, nor did it mean that the loss suddenly became acceptable.

Yet I sensed that I was in danger of allowing the deed to occupy more of my heart than it deserved.

There is a difference between acknowledging that someone has hurt us and allowing that hurt to become the permanent tenant of our minds.

In that stillness, I bowed my heart, asked for forgiveness for my unforgiveness, and released them.

It was not a declaration that nothing had happened. It was not an announcement that the damage no longer mattered. And it did not magically restore the trees.

It was a decision about what I would carry forward.

I could not command the past to change. I could not force the garden to rise again. I could not reach into another person's mind and demand an explanation that might never come.

But I could ask God to help me loosen my grip on the bitterness.

Forgiveness, I have come to understand, does not always arrive as a triumphant moment in which every wound closes and every question finds its answer. Sometimes it begins quietly, with a tired soul sitting beneath a carport, realising that anger has become another burden to carry.

And sometimes the first step towards freedom is simply asking God for the grace to take that step.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🕯️ The Woman Behind the Destruction.

The mastermind behind the deed was my ex-fiancée's mother—a woman whose personality, as I remember it, was defined by a shrill, banshee-like screech that used to pierce straight through the thick, double-layered brick walls characteristic of our South African homes.

She is gone now, carrying her secrets to the grave.

Whether her actions were rooted in dark superstition, calculated malice, a desire to exert control, or simply monumental ignorance, I will never truly know.

That uncertainty is one of the peculiar burdens of the past.

We can remember what happened. We can describe what we saw. We can speak honestly about the harm we experienced. But unless someone tells us what motivated them, we cannot always distinguish between the truth and the explanations our wounded minds construct to fill the silence.

I have sometimes wondered whether there was an intention behind the destruction that I never understood. Was it an act of spite? Was it an attempt to assert authority over a property and the people connected to it? Was there some private justification that seemed perfectly reasonable to the person who carried it out?

Or was the act simply the consequence of a person making a decision without caring enough to consider its consequences for somebody else?

These are questions, not verdicts.

The woman is no longer here to answer them. Whatever the truth might have been, I cannot interrogate a grave, and I will not pretend that speculation gives me access to the thoughts she took with her.

I can only describe the experience from where I stood: a man returning from work to discover that the garden he had known was gone.

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

## 🧠 What Might Drive Someone to Destroy a Garden?

Looking back, I have tried to understand how an act that appears so destructive and disproportionate might come about.

Human behaviour is complicated. People sometimes do things that seem incomprehensible to those who suffer the consequences. Psychology can offer useful ways of examining such behaviour, but it cannot reconstruct the motives of a particular person without evidence.

Several possibilities deserve consideration.

### 1. Spite, resentment, and the desire for control.

One possibility is that the destruction was connected to resentment or a desire to exercise control.

In strained family relationships, possessions, homes, gardens, and other meaningful surroundings can become symbols of independence, belonging, or personal identity. When conflict becomes entrenched, a person may target something valuable to another person as a way of expressing anger or demonstrating power.

If that was what happened, the garden may have represented far more than its individual trees. Destroying it would have changed a place that carried personal meaning and would have imposed the consequences of that decision on someone else.

However, the existence of family tension alone cannot establish that this was the motive. Without further evidence, deliberate spite remains a possibility rather than a proven explanation.

### 2. Ignorance, indifference, or an extraordinary failure of judgement.

Another possibility is that the person responsible failed to appreciate the significance of what was being destroyed.

People do not always value the same things. One person sees a mature fruit tree as the result of years of care; another sees an obstacle, an inconvenience, or something that can easily be replaced.

That difference does not automatically make an action malicious. But where a person has the power to make a decision affecting somebody else's property or cherished possessions, a failure to consider the consequences can cause serious harm.

A lack of understanding, combined with indifference to another person's feelings, may explain some destructive decisions without requiring a carefully planned scheme.

Yet ignorance, too, is only a possible explanation in this particular case. I have no evidence that would allow me to establish it as the truth.

### 3. Superstition, folklore, or unusual beliefs.

I have also wondered whether superstition played a part.

Across many societies, people have attached symbolic meanings to trees, gardens, boundaries, and the spaces in which they live. Some traditions associate the removal of plants or the alteration of a property with cleansing, protection, the severing of connections, or the removal of unwanted influences.

Such beliefs can influence how people interpret their surroundings and make decisions.

But there is an important distinction between recognising that superstitions exist and concluding that a particular person acted because of them.

I have no evidence that witchcraft, folk magic, or any particular belief motivated the destruction of my garden. Nor would the destruction itself establish the presence of a psychological disorder or an effective supernatural force.

The possibility remains part of my personal speculation, not an established fact.

### 4. Conflict that becomes larger than the original disagreement.

A final possibility is that the garden became entangled in a wider conflict.

When relationships deteriorate, an ordinary object or shared space can acquire a symbolic meaning far beyond its practical purpose. A decision that might otherwise have been discussed calmly can become an expression of resentment, rivalry, or the need to have the final word.

If that happened here, the trees may have become casualties of a conflict whose full dimensions I never understood.

But this explanation also has limits. I cannot identify a specific hidden dispute, reconstruct another person's reasoning, or claim to know what was said behind closed doors.

The honest conclusion is that several explanations are conceivable, but the evidence available to me does not establish which, if any, was responsible.

**Perhaps the hardest truth is that an act can leave us with lasting consequences even when its motive remains forever unknown.**

## 🌿 The Price of Rebuilding What Was Lost.

There is one more aspect of the experience that deserves attention: the difference between destroying something and rebuilding it.

Destruction can happen in a morning.

Restoration may take years.

A mature tree represents a relationship between time, soil, water, light, and patient care. Even if another tree is planted the following day, the original cannot simply be summoned back into existence.

The same is true of trust.

When a person damages a relationship, a home, or something another person has carefully nurtured, the immediate loss is only part of the consequence. There may be additional expenses, wasted effort, disruption, disappointment, and a long period during which the injured person must adapt to a changed reality.

There is also the emotional cost of knowing that something precious can be taken away without warning.

In my case, I was left with questions about the money that had gone into the garden and the years that would have been needed to replace its mature trees. But I was also left with the knowledge that the garden I had known belonged to a chapter of my life that could not be recreated in precisely the same form.

I could have planted another lemon tree. I could have bought another rose bush. I could have begun again.

But beginning again is not the same as never having suffered the loss.

Perhaps that is why the memory remains so vivid. The destruction was visible in the stumps, but the full cost was spread across time: in the lost seasons, the missing shade, the vanished fruit, and the questions that never received an answer.

The garden had been growing quietly while I lived my life.

Its destruction interrupted that quiet continuity.

And rebuilding, whether in a garden or in the human heart, requires a patience that destruction itself does not demand.

## 🕊️ Forgiveness Does Not Rewrite History.

Twenty-one years later, I do not need to pretend that the event was insignificant in order to move forward.

The trees were there.

The trees were destroyed.

I returned home and experienced a profound shock.

Those facts belong to my memory, and forgiveness does not require me to erase them.

What has changed is the way I choose to hold the memory.

I can remember without seeking revenge. I can acknowledge the harm without allowing hatred to become the central feature of the story. I can admit that I still have unanswered questions without inventing answers merely to satisfy my curiosity.

And I can forgive without claiming that the deed was justified.

That distinction matters.

Forgiveness is not a declaration that wrongdoing does not matter. It is not an obligation to deny the consequences of another person's actions. Nor does it automatically restore trust or require someone to resume a relationship that has become harmful.

It is possible to release bitterness while retaining an honest account of what happened.

It is possible to grieve what was lost while refusing to let the loss dictate every thought that follows.

And it is possible to leave a question unanswered because the person who could have answered it is no longer here.

For me, the quiet moment beneath the carport remains central to this story. I believe God met me there, not by returning the trees, but by drawing my attention to the bitterness that was taking root within me.

The garden had been stripped of its growth.

I did not want my own heart to suffer the same fate.

## 🌅 What Remains After Twenty-One Years?

When I look back now, I see more than a collection of stumps and a ruined garden.

I see a younger version of myself, already carrying the grief of losing my mother, returning from an ordinary day at work to discover an extraordinary act of destruction.

I see the shock, the fury, the questions about money and wasted years, and the helplessness of standing before something that could not be repaired by wishing it had never happened.

I also remember the stillness that followed, and the conviction that I needed to release the bitterness rather than carry it indefinitely.

I cannot tell you with certainty why the garden was destroyed. I cannot establish whether the decision was motivated by spite, indifference, superstition, or something else entirely.

I can only tell you what the experience meant to me and what I have continued to consider in the years since.

The trees are gone.

The woman who, in my understanding, was responsible is gone.

The questions remain, but they no longer have to rule the story.

Perhaps that is what survival sometimes looks like. Not the recovery of everything we have lost, and not the discovery of every hidden motive, but the gradual ability to look back without allowing the past to consume the present.

Standing amidst the stumps, the anger eventually dissolved into history, leaving only the quiet dignity of survival.

And somewhere beyond the memory of that destroyed garden lies a lesson I am still learning:

**We cannot always choose what others destroy, but with God's help, we can choose what we allow to take root within us.**

🌳 Some trees never grow back.

🕊️ Some questions never find answers.

❤️ But even after devastation, the human heart can learn to grow again.

## 📝 Author's Note.

This is a personal recollection written approximately twenty-one years after the events described. The account reflects my memories and understanding of that time.

The discussion of possible motives is reflective analysis, not a psychological assessment of the people involved. I cannot establish the precise reasons for the destruction, and I have distinguished my suspicions from facts I personally remember.

I offer this piece as part of a wider autobiographical journey through grief, conflict, faith, and forgiveness.
