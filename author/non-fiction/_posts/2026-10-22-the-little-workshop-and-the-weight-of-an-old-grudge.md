# 🛠️ The Little Workshop and the Weight of an Old Grudge.

Some dreams are never finished. Others are abandoned halfway through, leaving us to spend years wondering what might have been.

When I was about fourteen, my father began building me a workshop of my own. It was to be a modest corrugated-iron lean-to, attached to his brick workshop — an old, converted servant's quarters tucked into the southwest corner of our backyard.

To me, it was a palace in the making.

There would be a window to catch the afternoon light, a proper door, and, best of all, a set of keys that belonged to me. I imagined a place where I could work with my hands, learn my craft, and share something of my father's world.

The timber frame was taking shape. The dream, it seemed, was finally becoming real.

## 🔨 A Joke, a Hammer, and a Sudden Silence.

One day, as we worked on the door section, a neighbourhood boy stopped by to watch. He lived in the yard behind our lane, and his father had, at one time, been a drinking companion of my dad.

There was nothing unusual about his presence. Boys wandered in and out of one another's lives, and a bit of teasing was part of the territory.

Looking at our handiwork, my friend grinned and said to my father:

"Uncle, you must make the door small so that me and little Otto can run in there and hide from you when we're doing naughty things."

I laughed.

It was an ordinary boyhood joke, the sort that disappears into the afternoon almost as soon as it is spoken.

But my father did not laugh.

He took the remark literally, and the atmosphere changed. The pride and purpose that had accompanied our work gave way to anger. He put down his tools, and the building stopped.

Just like that.

The hammer fell silent. The door remained unfinished. And a boy's innocent attempt at humour became the end of something I had eagerly anticipated.

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🕰️ The Dream Left in the Frame.

I could not understand it. Later, when I asked my father why he had abandoned the project, his answer cut deeper than I expected.

He told me he had lost all interest because of what my friend had said — and because I had laughed along with him.

I was fourteen. I had laughed at a joke, not at my father. But the distinction meant little to him that day.

A year or two later, my father became ill. The workshop was never completed.

I cannot say what might have happened had that afternoon unfolded differently. Perhaps the building would have been finished. Perhaps I would have spent years working in that little space, surrounded by the smell of timber, the rasp of a file, and the satisfying music of tools at work.

Instead, the project remained an unfinished promise.

And somewhere in the years that followed, I began to associate that lost dream with the boy who had made the joke.

It was a peculiar thing to carry for so long: resentment toward someone whose words had lasted only seconds, while their imagined consequences remained with me for years.

## 💭 The Burden I Carried.

Looking back, I recognise how easily a childhood memory can become a story we keep telling ourselves.

In mine, my friend had spoiled something precious. His joke had changed the course of the afternoon, and the workshop had never risen beyond its unfinished frame.

For years, that was the version I carried.

Yet time has a way of exposing the cracks in our old explanations. My friend had been a boy, making a joke. He had not set out to destroy my dream, offend my father, or leave a wound that would follow me into adulthood.

My father made his own decision that day.

That does not erase my disappointment, nor does it make the memory less painful. It simply places responsibility where it belongs. A careless remark may provoke a reaction, but the reaction is not always the speaker's burden to carry.

I had allowed the boy's joke to occupy a place in my heart far longer than it had ever occupied a place in his mind.

Perhaps he forgot the entire incident before the sun went down.

I did not.

## 🕊️ Learning to Lay It Down.

Forgiveness is not a claim that nothing hurt. Nor does it require us to pretend that the past unfolded as it should have.

Sometimes forgiveness begins with a quieter admission: that we have carried something long enough.

The workshop could not be completed in retrospect. My father's illness could not be undone. The years could not return that afternoon to us, tools in hand, with the door still waiting to be fitted.

But I could reconsider the resentment I had attached to my friend.

He was a boy. I was a boy. Neither of us could have understood how a fleeting joke might become entangled with disappointment, illness, and the loss of a dream.

I do not need to rewrite the memory to find peace with it. I need only to stop asking that boy to answer, all these years later, for an ending he did not choose.

The little workshop was never finished. But perhaps there is still something to be built from its memory — a little more understanding, a little less bitterness, and room in the heart for forgiveness.

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## 🌅 What Remains.

Somewhere in the landscape of my childhood, that workshop still stands.

Not in timber or corrugated iron, but in memory: a frame without a finished door, a hammer laid aside, and a fourteen-year-old boy discovering that grown-ups can abandon dreams for reasons children struggle to understand.

For years, I remembered the joke.

Now, I would rather remember the dream — and the lesson that came from losing it.

We cannot always finish what we begin. We cannot always recover what illness, anger, or time takes away. But we can choose what we continue carrying.

And I no longer wish to carry that old grudge.

Some doors remain unfinished. Others, at last, we learn to close gently behind us.

✍️ **Otto Brink**
