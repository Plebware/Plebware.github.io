# 🚆 Chapter Three — From Railway Engineering to Computing.

*An eleven-year journey through railway engineering, practical problem-solving, and the foundations of a lifelong relationship with technology.*

<!-- PLEBVOX:END -->

<!-- PLEBVOX:START -->

## The Railway Years — Learning Through Practical Work. 🚆

Before PlebWare, before Linux, and long before artificial intelligence became part of everyday conversation, Otto Brinkmeier spent approximately eleven years working on the South African railways.

It was a substantial chapter of his working life. He spent about seven years at the Electrical Train Sheds in Braamfontein, followed by the remainder of his railway career at Benrose Depot, near City Deep.

The railway environment was a world of practical engineering. Equipment had to work. Faults had to be identified, problems understood, and repairs carried out. It was not enough to look at a malfunctioning piece of equipment and hope it would start behaving again. Someone had to investigate what was wrong.

That kind of work rewards a particular approach to problems:

- Observe what is happening.
- Identify what is not working.
- Investigate the possible causes.
- Understand the system before attempting a repair.
- Test the result.

These principles would remain important throughout Otto's later life, even when the systems he worked with were no longer railway equipment.

His railway experience was not simply a job on a CV. It was practical education in how complicated systems behave, how individual components depend on one another, and why understanding a problem is often more important than rushing towards a solution.

## Braamfontein and Benrose — More Than Just a Workplace. 🔧

The railway years were not made up entirely of machinery, maintenance, and technical responsibilities. Like any workplace, they also had their routines, characters, friendships, and little adventures.

There were lunch breaks, conversations, and familiar places. Otto remembers buying chips and Russians — the South African sausage, not people from Russia! — and visiting Akawela's Café, where Bomber Jack was part of the familiar surroundings.

There were also occasions when a lunch break became an opportunity for a little competition. Otto remembers sometimes finding time to challenge another railway worker's high score on a game.

He also recalls walking to the plaza and knowing Mohammed, who sold car sound equipment. That small detail would become relevant to another interest that occupied his attention: car audio.

These memories matter because a working life is not just a list of positions held and duties performed. It is also made up of the ordinary moments that make a place familiar.

The railway provided a livelihood, but it also gave Otto years of experience among people, equipment, and everyday challenges.

## The Engineer's Mindset — Understanding Before Repairing. ⚙️

There is a connection between Otto's railway experience and the way he approaches computers today.

At first glance, electrical railway equipment and computer systems might seem like two entirely different worlds. Their components, technologies, and applications certainly differ. But the underlying discipline of troubleshooting has much in common.

A fault in a railway system cannot always be solved by replacing the first component that looks suspicious. A computer problem cannot always be solved by reinstalling the operating system. In both cases, the person doing the work needs to understand what the system is supposed to do, what it is actually doing, and where the difference lies.

This is the sort of thinking that would later influence Otto's approach to Linux, software, and the development of PlebMachine.

He prefers to investigate, experiment, and understand rather than blindly follow instructions. He wants to know why something works, not merely which button to press.

That attitude has its advantages. It can also mean taking the longer route, because understanding a system properly sometimes requires patience.

But it is a recurring feature of Otto's technical journey: curiosity leads to investigation, investigation leads to understanding, and understanding opens the door to building something of his own.

## The Sound of Another Interest — Car Audio. 🔊

The railway years were not the only place where Otto's technical interests found an outlet.

He also became interested in car audio, and this was more than simply buying a radio and turning up the volume.

He remembers owning a Pioneer front-loading CD system, accompanied by two twelve-inch Pioneer subwoofers. Later, in a Toyota Corolla installation, he used a Tornado amplifier and two ten-inch subwoofers.

The Corolla installation involved considerably more than connecting a few wires. There was a fibreglass enclosure built around the spare-wheel area, a wooden frame, and a false floor that allowed components to be concealed.

It was a practical project involving design, space, materials, wiring, and sound.

Otto recalls that the system reached approximately 130 decibels, although that figure is a recollection rather than a verified measurement.

The interesting part of this memory is not simply how loud the system could play. It is the willingness to work out how to build an installation that fitted the vehicle, made use of the available space, and delivered the desired result.

Once again, the same practical instinct was at work: understand the components, consider how they fit together, and create something that achieves a purpose.

## The Computer Was Already Waiting in the Background. 💻

There was another thread running through Otto's life during this period: his interest in computers.

In 1982, at the age of nineteen, he encountered the Commodore VIC-20 and Microsoft BASIC. This was an important early experience. A computer was no longer just an abstract piece of technology. It was something that could be instructed, experimented with, and used to make things happen.

The idea of programming introduced a different kind of problem-solving. Instead of tracing a fault through electrical or mechanical equipment, one could give a machine a set of instructions and observe the result.

A small mistake in an instruction could produce an unexpected outcome. A correctly structured program could perform a task repeatedly and consistently.

For someone who enjoyed understanding how things worked, this opened another door.

The VIC-20 experience is explored further in the earlier article, [Teenage Dreams! — Before the Idea, It Was There](https://plebware.github.io/everyday/news/2026/09/11/before-idea-it-was-there/).

The important point is that Otto's interest in computing did not suddenly appear when Linux became popular or when the internet made software development more accessible. The curiosity had been there for decades.

## From Electrical Systems to Digital Systems. 🔑

It would be convenient to describe Otto's journey as a neat, direct progression from railway engineering into computer programming. Real lives rarely follow such tidy diagrams.

The railway years, his early computer interests, his practical projects, and his later business ambitions were parts of a much longer process. They did not necessarily happen in a straight line, and the precise timeline deserves to be treated honestly rather than filled with convenient assumptions.

What can be seen clearly, however, is the continuity in his way of thinking.

The electrical fitter who wanted to understand why a piece of equipment was not working became the computer user who wanted to understand why an operating system behaved in a particular way.

The person who could plan a complicated car-audio installation became someone willing to investigate software, experiment with tools, and build a technical environment around his own requirements.

The technologies changed. The curiosity did not.

This is also why Linux became such a natural environment for Otto. It offers the opportunity to look beneath the surface, make choices, learn how components interact, and take responsibility for the system being built.

His later work with Linux and PlebMachine reflects that same approach. Rather than accepting that technology must be used exactly as someone else designed it, he wants to understand it and adapt it to serve a practical purpose.

## The Lessons That Stayed. 🧠

Looking back, the railway years contributed more than technical experience.

They were years of working within a large operational environment, learning through practical responsibilities, and dealing with systems that had to function reliably.

Several lessons carried forward:

1. **Understand the system.** A problem is easier to solve when the person investigating it understands how the parts work together.
2. **Do not rush the diagnosis.** An incorrect assumption can lead to unnecessary work and a poor solution.
3. **Learn by doing.** Reading and theory have value, but practical experimentation turns knowledge into experience.
4. **Make things serve a purpose.** A technical solution is only worthwhile when it addresses a real need.
5. **Keep asking questions.** Curiosity is not something that belongs exclusively to youth. It can remain useful throughout an entire lifetime.

These principles would eventually become part of the thinking behind PlebWare: technology should not exist merely to impress people. It should make life easier, solve problems, and serve humanity.

The railway years were an important foundation, but they were not the final destination.

The idea of independence and building something of his own would become increasingly important.

But becoming an entrepreneur is not simply a matter of having an idea. It requires knowledge, planning, money, timing, and the ability to deal with setbacks. Sometimes enthusiasm and technical ability are not enough.
