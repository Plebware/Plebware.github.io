# When Knowing Isn't Enough — And Why Living It Matters.

There are some problems in life that cannot be solved by having the right answer.

You can have the right advice.

You can have the right information.

You can have experience, education, patience, compassion and even faith.

And sometimes, despite all of that, the situation remains incredibly difficult.

This is particularly true when dealing with addiction, destructive behaviour, broken relationships and people who are unwilling — or unable — to change.

There is another uncomfortable truth.

We cannot control other people.

We can advise them.

We can warn them.

We can encourage them.

We can listen to them.

We can love them.

We can pray for them.

But we cannot climb inside another person's mind and make them choose differently.

And perhaps learning to accept that is one of the hardest lessons in human relationships.

# The Person We Cannot Control.

When somebody we love is behaving destructively, our natural instinct is to try to fix the problem.

We look for solutions.

We search for rehabilitation centres.

We make appointments.

We issue warnings.

We offer advice.

We plead.

Sometimes we become angry.

Sometimes we become frustrated.

Sometimes we say things we later regret.

And sometimes we simply become exhausted.

The problem becomes even more complicated when addiction is involved.

Substance addiction does not affect only the person taking the substance. It can affect an entire household — finances, relationships, employment, health, safety and the emotional wellbeing of everyone around the addicted person.

South Africa faces its own serious challenges in this area. Treatment capacity is limited, and relapse remains a major problem. Research and reporting from South African treatment programmes show that returning to substance use after treatment is sadly common, while newer approaches that combine medication, counselling and continuing social support have shown promising results. [The Guardian, "Heroin hustle: drug crisis project helping South African addicts."](https://www.theguardian.com/global-development/2024/sep/13/heroin-hustle-drug-crisis-project-helping-south-african-addicts)

That tells us something important.

Recovery is rarely as simple as putting somebody into rehabilitation and expecting them to emerge permanently changed.

Human beings are considerably more complicated than that.

# Two Ears And One Mouth.

There is an old saying that God gave us two ears and one mouth because we should listen twice as much as we speak.

Whether or not somebody regards that as theology, it is excellent advice.

Listening is one of the most underrated skills in dealing with difficult people.

We often listen while preparing our answer.

We listen for the opportunity to correct somebody.

We listen for the flaw in their argument.

We listen because we want our turn to speak.

But genuine listening is different.

Genuine listening says:

"I hear you."

"It sounds like this is difficult for you."

"Help me understand."

"I may not agree with you, but I want to understand where you're coming from."

That does not mean approving of destructive behaviour.

It does not mean accepting manipulation.

It does not mean abandoning boundaries.

It simply means recognising that there is a human being on the other side of the conversation.

And sometimes that is the first step towards getting anywhere at all.

# Empathy Is Not Apathy.

There is an enormous difference between empathy and apathy.

Apathy says:

"I don't care."

Empathy says:

"I may not agree with you, but I want to understand what you are experiencing."

That distinction matters.

If we want to communicate effectively with difficult people, we have to make an effort to understand the world from their perspective.

Why are they angry?

Why are they frightened?

Why are they behaving defensively?

Why do they keep making the same destructive decisions?

What happened to them?

What are they trying to escape?

What are they trying to find?

These questions don't excuse harmful behaviour.

Understanding is not the same thing as approval.

But understanding can change the way we respond.

A person who feels constantly attacked will often defend themselves.

A person who feels heard may eventually become willing to listen.

And sometimes the greatest communication skill is not finding the perfect thing to say.

It is knowing when to say nothing.

# Sometimes Silence Is Wisdom.

We live in a world that expects an immediate response to everything.

Somebody says something.

We answer.

Somebody insults us.

We retaliate.

Somebody makes a mistake.

We explain why they are wrong.

Somebody behaves badly.

We tell them exactly what we think.

But there are moments when the wisest response is simply:

"I hear you."

"I need to think about that."

"That is difficult."

"Let me consider what you've said."

Silence does not always mean weakness.

Sometimes silence is self-control.

Sometimes it is compassion.

Sometimes it is wisdom.

And sometimes it gives another person something that arguments rarely provide:

The opportunity to think.

# The Difference Between Knowing And Living.

It is possible to know the right thing without doing the right thing.

We know we should be patient.

We know we should listen.

We know we should communicate properly.

We know we should control our temper.

We know forgiveness is important.

We know compassion matters.

We know that anger often makes a difficult situation worse.

Yet knowing all of those things does not automatically make us capable of doing them.

There is a difference between knowledge in the head and truth in the heart.

The nineteenth-century Christian writer J. C. Ryle repeatedly returned to this distinction.

In *Holiness*, Ryle challenged the idea that outward religious knowledge, profession, morality or even enthusiasm were sufficient evidence of genuine Christian character. For him, true Christianity had to reach the heart and become visible in the life. [J. C. Ryle, *Holiness*, via Christian Classics Ethereal Library.](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/ryle/holiness.iii.xiv.html)

That principle extends far beyond religion.

Knowing what patience is doesn't make us patient.

Knowing what compassion is doesn't make us compassionate.

Knowing how to communicate doesn't mean we communicate well when we are angry.

Knowledge is important.

But knowledge has to become practice.

# The Christian Has An Even Harder Road.

For Christians, the challenge becomes even greater.

We are not merely called to know what Jesus taught.

We are called to follow Him.

That sounds wonderfully simple until somebody hurts us.

Then it becomes difficult.

Jesus tells His followers to love their enemies.

Pray for those who persecute them.

Turn the other cheek.

Forgive.

Show mercy.

Speak truth in love.

Do good without demanding recognition.

These are beautiful teachings when we discuss them in a Bible study.

They become considerably more challenging when we are standing in the middle of a family crisis, exhausted, angry and frightened.

That is where Christianity stops being theoretical.

It becomes practical.

It becomes personal.

It becomes a daily battle between what our emotions want to do and what our faith tells us we should do.

This is one of the reasons Ryle's emphasis on practical holiness remains so relevant. He argued that genuine faith cannot remain merely a matter of knowledge or profession; it must work its way into the way we actually live. [J. C. Ryle, *Holiness*, via Christian Classics Ethereal Library.](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/ryle/holiness.iii.iii.html)

It is one thing to know the words.

It is another thing entirely to live them.

# Ambassadors, Not Controllers.

Christians are called to be ambassadors for Christ.

An ambassador represents somebody else.

That means our behaviour matters.

When we lose our temper, people see us.

When we show compassion, people see us.

When we forgive, people see us.

When we refuse to retaliate, people see us.

When we remain patient under pressure, people see us.

That does not mean Christians should become doormats.

There is a difference between turning the other cheek and allowing somebody to destroy a household.

There is a difference between forgiveness and removing every consequence of somebody's behaviour.

There is a difference between loving someone and enabling their addiction.

Boundaries can be necessary.

Protection can be necessary.

Professional intervention can be necessary.

But even while establishing those boundaries, Christians are still called to examine their own hearts.

The question is not only:

"How do I get this person to change?"

It is also:

"How does God want me to respond?"

That second question is often much harder.

# Happiness Is Not The Same As Pleasure.

Perhaps this brings us to the bigger question.

What are human beings actually looking for?

Most people would probably say they want happiness.

But we often confuse happiness with pleasure.

We pursue money.

Relationships.

Sex.

Drugs.

Alcohol.

Entertainment.

Possessions.

Status.

Power.

Approval.

And there is nothing inherently wrong with every one of these things in its proper place.

The problem begins when we expect them to provide something they were never designed to provide.

Temporary pleasure can be mistaken for lasting happiness.

Escape can be mistaken for freedom.

Intoxication can be mistaken for peace.

Attention can be mistaken for love.

Possessions can be mistaken for security.

Control can be mistaken for strength.

Yet the more desperately we chase these things, the more desperately we can discover that they don't satisfy us.

Perhaps genuine happiness is not primarily about getting everything we want.

Perhaps it is about becoming the kind of person who can live meaningfully even when life doesn't give us everything we want.

# The One Percent.

There is another kind of "one percent" that interests me.

Not the richest one percent.

Not the most successful one percent.

Not the most powerful one percent.

I mean the people who actually practise what they claim to believe.

The people who don't merely talk about patience but practise it.

Who don't merely talk about forgiveness but forgive.

Who don't merely talk about compassion but show compassion.

Who don't merely talk about listening but actually listen.

Who don't merely talk about faith but continue trusting God when everything around them appears to be falling apart.

For the Christian, this is where the difference between head knowledge and heart knowledge becomes particularly important.

It is easy to say:

"I trust God."

It is considerably harder to trust Him when the situation appears impossible.

It is easy to say:

"I forgive."

It is much harder to forgive somebody who has hurt you repeatedly.

It is easy to say:

"I will pray for them."

It is much harder to pray sincerely for somebody who has caused tremendous pain.

That is where faith becomes real.

Not when life is easy.

When life is difficult.

# We Cannot Save Everybody.

There is another lesson that is painful but necessary.

We cannot save everybody.

We cannot rescue everybody.

We cannot make everybody understand.

We cannot force somebody into recovery.

We cannot make somebody accept our advice.

We cannot make somebody accept our faith.

We cannot make somebody love us.

What we can do is control our own response.

We can listen.

We can communicate.

We can show compassion.

We can establish boundaries.

We can seek professional help.

We can protect vulnerable people.

We can refuse to participate in destructive behaviour.

And, if we are people of faith, we can pray.

Then we have to leave room for something greater than ourselves.

Because sometimes the greatest act of humility is admitting:

"I cannot fix this."

# When All You Can Do Is Pray.

Prayer is sometimes misunderstood as doing nothing.

It isn't.

Prayer can be the place where we acknowledge that we have reached the limits of our own ability.

It is where anger can become surrender.

Fear can become trust.

Desperation can become hope.

And sometimes prayer changes the person we are praying for.

Sometimes it changes the circumstances.

And sometimes — perhaps most surprisingly — it changes us.

The Christian who enters a prayer room angry may leave with patience.

The person who enters frustrated may leave with compassion.

The person who enters wanting somebody else to change may leave asking God to change their own heart.

That doesn't make the problem disappear.

But it can change the person standing in the middle of the problem.

And sometimes that is where the first real change begins.

# Difficult People Can Teach Us Difficult Lessons.

Perhaps difficult people enter our lives for reasons we would never choose.

They can teach us patience.

They can expose our anger.

They can reveal our pride.

They can show us how quickly we judge.

They can force us to learn communication.

They can teach us to listen.

They can make us discover compassion.

And, for Christians, they can expose the difference between knowing what Jesus taught and actually attempting to live it.

That does not mean we should romanticise destructive behaviour.

Addiction is serious.

Abuse is serious.

Manipulation is serious.

Mental and physical safety matter.

Families need support.

Professional intervention can be essential.

But somewhere within all of that difficulty is a question worth asking:

**Who am I becoming while I am dealing with this?**

That may be one of the most important questions we can ask.

# The Hardest Kind Of Knowledge.

Perhaps, ultimately, the hardest knowledge isn't knowing what is right.

It is knowing what is right — and then doing it when everything inside you wants to do something else.

Be patient when you want to explode.

Listen when you want to lecture.

Stay silent when you want the last word.

Show compassion when you feel none.

Set boundaries without hatred.

Forgive without pretending the damage never happened.

Seek help without shame.

Pray when you would rather give up.

And keep walking.

For the Christian, that is where faith moves from the head into the heart.

For everyone else, perhaps it is simply the discovery that wisdom is not measured by how much we know.

It is measured by what we do with what we know.

Because knowing isn't enough.

Eventually, we have to live it.
