# 75 Percent A Writer

When a man sits down to write with a heavy heart, he may be tired of the daily grind. His headache is real, his budget is little more than a concept on paper, and all around him are sighs, mumbling, and grumbling.

From the mouths of those around him come words such as, "We are tired," and, "We cannot live like this anymore."

Those words do little to lift his spirits.

He knows he must seek his God, for that is where his help comes from.

But alas, however hard he seeks, there is nothing but silence.

No! Not utter silence, perhaps. Only the echoes of his own painful cries, travelling back and forth through the corridors of his tormented mind.

And so, this author wanders.

## Corridor One: Did I Make a Mistake by Voting Yes?

One thought that has occupied this author's mind is the memory of having voted **YES**.

Was it a mistake to want democracy?

The day was Tuesday, 17 March 1992, the date of South Africa's last whites-only referendum.

It became known as the 1992 Apartheid Referendum.

### The Question on the Ballot.

Voters were asked, in English and Afrikaans:

> Do you support continuation of the reform process which the State President began on 2 February 1990 and which is aimed at a new Constitution through negotiation?

> Ondersteun u die voortsetting van die hervormingsproses wat die Staatspresident op 2 Februarie 1990 begin het en wat op 'n nuwe grondwet deur onderhandeling gemik is?

There were only two choices.

**YES or NO.**

### What Did a YES Mean?

President F. W. de Klerk called the referendum at a time when his government was losing white-only by-elections to the Conservative Party, which opposed the reform process and wanted to preserve apartheid. De Klerk put his own position on the line, declaring that he would resign if the voters rejected his course.

A YES vote supported three important things:

1. **A mandate for reform.** De Klerk had already unbanned the African National Congress and the South African Communist Party and released Nelson Mandela and other political prisoners in 1990.

2. **Continued negotiations.** The result strengthened the government's mandate to pursue negotiations towards a new constitutional arrangement, including the process associated with CODESA.

3. **A step towards ending apartheid.** The referendum gave De Klerk political backing to continue negotiations that helped pave the way for South Africa's first democratic national election in April 1994.

The YES vote won 68.73% of valid votes.

De Klerk famously declared the following day:

> Today we have closed the book on apartheid.

Yet there was a painful contradiction in that historic moment.

Black South Africans, the people most profoundly affected by apartheid, had no vote in the referendum. The white minority was deciding whether to support negotiations towards a new political future that would ultimately include the people it had excluded from political power.

Ignorance, prejudice, and fear were deeply rooted in our society. The consequences of our history have not simply disappeared.

### The Numbers Behind the Decision.

The turnout was substantial for a whites-only referendum.

- Registered voters: 3,296,800.
- Total votes cast: 2,804,947.
- Turnout: 85.08%.
- Valid votes: 2,799,805.
- Invalid or blank votes: 5,142.

The final result was:

- **YES:** 1,924,186 votes — 68.73%.
- **NO:** 875,619 votes — 31.27%.

Only one of the fifteen voting regions, Pietersburg in the then Northern Transvaal, returned a majority NO vote.

Pretoria, despite expectations of stronger conservative opposition, voted YES. Cape Town recorded approximately 85% support.

These figures are documented in the historical account published by [South African History Online](https://sahistory.org.za/article/1992-whites-only-referendum-or-against-negotiated-constitution).

So, when people like me say, "We voted YES for democracy," we are referring to that 68.73% of white voters who supported the continuation of negotiations on 17 March 1992.

That vote did not, by itself, create democracy. It provided a political mandate for negotiations that, alongside the struggles and decisions of many South Africans, helped lead to the democratic election of 27 April 1994.

It hurts this author to feel vilified by a government that, in his view, repeatedly invokes apartheid when explaining present-day failures.

I recognise the damage apartheid caused. I also believe that acknowledging the past should not prevent us from asking difficult questions about the present.

But let me back out of this pain-filled corridor.

## Corridor Two: The Financial Hallway.

Let us turn into the hallway of financial hardship.

From there, another door opens: the pensions corridor.

I have been receiving a SASSA old-age grant since 2023. Yet I believe I may qualify for a military-veterans pension instead.

Oh, woe is me!

Or should I say, 💭 *oui, wey?*

A little humour sometimes helps a man keep walking when the corridor seems to stretch endlessly before him.

This is a hallway in which many former servicemen find themselves stuck. My old military buddies and I are not alone.

For years, some of us have struggled to find the right route through the bureaucracy. Part of the problem is that people use similar names for different pension arrangements.

### Two Different Military Pension Arrangements.

South Africa has more than one military-related pension system, and it is important not to confuse them.

The SASSA War Veterans Grant is a specific social-assistance grant with historical service-related eligibility requirements. It is not a general pension for everyone who served in the South African Defence Force during the Border War.

That distinction matters to former SADF servicemen like me.

The pension arrangement I need to investigate is the **Military Veterans Pension Benefit** administered through the Department of Military Veterans framework.

### The Department of Military Veterans.

The Military Veterans Act 18 of 2011 established a framework for recognising and supporting qualifying military veterans and their dependants.

The Department of Military Veterans, commonly known as the DMV, deals with military-veteran recognition and benefits. The Government Pensions Administration Agency also has a role in administering the Military Veterans Pension Benefit.

The relevant government framework recognises qualifying former members of specified statutory and non-statutory forces. However, eligibility depends on the legal definition of a military veteran, qualifying service, registration, and the applicable benefit requirements.

This is why I must establish my eligibility through the official process rather than assume that previous service alone guarantees a pension.

The benefit framework includes pension support for qualifying veterans, subject to the applicable regulations. Other veteran-support programmes address areas such as healthcare, housing, education, and burial assistance, where the relevant eligibility requirements are met.

The official starting points are:

- [Department of Military Veterans](https://www.dmv.gov.za/).
- [Government Pensions Administration Agency — Military Veterans Pensions](https://www.gpaa.gov.za/mvp.html).
- [South African Government — amended Military Veterans Pension regulations announcement, February 2026](https://www.gov.za/news/media-statements/military-veterans-amended-regulations-military-veterans-pension-benefit-27).

### Why Do My Buddies and I Keep Hitting a Wall?

Several practical obstacles may stand between a former serviceman and a completed application.

**1. Missing registration.**

A veteran may have served but still need to establish or confirm his registration on the appropriate national military-veterans database.

**2. Missing service records.**

Service certificates, discharge documents, and archival records may be difficult to locate decades after the events in question.

**3. Disputed or incomplete service details.**

A force number, service dates, or other identifying details may need verification.

**4. Being sent from one office to another.**

One office may direct an applicant elsewhere, leaving him caught between institutions without a clear answer.

For an older man living on a limited income, each additional journey, document request, or unanswered telephone call can become another obstacle.

The result is exhausting.

### What I Am Actually Trying to Do.

My immediate objective is not to claim that I have already qualified.

**My objective is to establish my military-veteran registration and find out exactly what is required to submit a valid pension application.**

The documents I understand may be required include:

- South African identity document.
- Proof of military service and discharge, where available.
- An affidavit explaining missing service documents, if the relevant authority accepts it.
- Proof of whether I receive another state pension or social grant.
- Recent bank statements.
- Proof of residential address.
- Any additional registration or verification documents requested by the responsible authority.

I have my identity document, but locating my old service and discharge papers is a different matter. I may need to obtain archival confirmation or provide an affidavit, depending on the official requirements.

I must also establish how my existing SASSA old-age grant affects any application. The rules governing other pensions and social grants matter, and I should not assume that a military-veterans pension will automatically replace or supplement my current income.

That is the task ahead: finding the right office, establishing my records, and getting a clear answer.

It is a practical task, but it is also a deeply personal one.

For a man who once served his country, having to prove his own history decades later can feel like being asked to explain a chapter that should never have been forgotten.

This corridor of my twisted and agonised mind is becoming too painful to remain in for much longer.

## Where to From Here?

And so I find myself standing at another junction.

One corridor leads towards PlebWare, where there is still work to be done, ideas to develop, and knowledge to share.

Another leads towards moonlighting, with a broken vehicle complicating the effort to make ends meet.

And yet another leads deeper into the corridors of my mind, where memories, disappointments, questions, and unfinished thoughts wait for their turn to be written down.

Perhaps that is what being seventy-five per cent a writer means.

A man may have the thoughts, the memories, the desire to create, and even the words. But the other twenty-five per cent must somehow find the time, the strength, the money, and the peace of mind to bring those words into the world.

Some days, even that final quarter feels like climbing a mountain.

Still, the corridors remain open.

There is another story to tell, another problem to solve, another page to write.

And perhaps, somewhere along the way, the silence will give way to an answer.

For now, I shall take a breath, gather my thoughts, and decide which door to open next.

**PlebWare, moonlighting with a broken vehicle, or more painful thoughts to put on paper?**

That, dear reader, is the question facing this writer today.
