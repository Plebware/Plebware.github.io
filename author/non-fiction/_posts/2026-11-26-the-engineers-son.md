# 🔧 Chapter One — The Engineer's Son.

*Before there was PlebWare, before there was a computer, before there was a website to build, there was a boy who wanted to know how things worked.*

Every story has a beginning.

Sometimes it begins with a defining moment. Sometimes it begins with a person whose influence remains long after childhood has passed. And sometimes it begins with something as ordinary as a book lying on a shelf.

In my case, one of the strongest influences was my father.

He was an engineer, a man with two master's degrees: one in mechanical engineering and the other in electrical engineering. To me, he was much more than a qualified professional. He was my ultimate role model.

I did not necessarily understand the full extent of his knowledge when I was young. But I understood that he knew how things worked, and that fascinated me.

Looking back, I can see that my interest in technology did not suddenly appear when I discovered computers. Its roots were planted much earlier.

They began in the home of an engineer.

## 👨‍🔧 A Father Who Set the Example.

My father's qualifications represented years of study, discipline and technical understanding. Mechanical engineering and electrical engineering are two different but closely connected worlds. One deals with the physical principles of machines and mechanisms; the other explores electricity, circuits and the systems that make modern technology possible.

I grew up knowing that my father understood both.

He was my ultimate role model, and that is an important part of this story. His influence was not simply about the career I might eventually choose. It was also about the world he introduced me to: a world in which complicated things could be examined, understood and, perhaps, built.

But there was another side to my father that mattered just as much as his engineering knowledge.

He was a very good man. If somebody telephoned him at two o'clock in the morning because they needed help, he would get out of bed and go. He did not seem to measure kindness by convenience or by the hour on the clock. If someone needed him, he tried to be there.

That example taught me something about compassion, generosity, loyalty and responsibility. It showed me that a person's abilities are not only there to advance their own interests. They can also be used to help other people.

My father was Lutheran and believed in God. He always tried to guide me towards God, and those efforts, together with the values he demonstrated through the way he treated people, played a very big role in my life.

My own journey has had its twists and turns. I have not always followed the path as consistently as he might have hoped. But the influence was planted early, and it remained part of me. His example gave me more than an interest in engineering; it helped shape my understanding of how a person should treat others and the importance of faith.

A machine was not just a machine.

It was a collection of components, each performing a function. There were reasons behind the way things were designed. There were mechanisms to investigate, electrical principles to understand and problems that could be approached logically.

That world appealed to me.

I was not yet thinking about careers, businesses or making a living from technology. I was simply fascinated by what was possible.

## 📚 The Books That Opened a World.

My father's technical library was a constant attraction.

There were books about engineering, machinery and technical subjects. For a young mind interested in how things worked, it was a treasure chest.

One publication that particularly captured my imagination was *Popular Mechanics*.

Its pages introduced me to inventions, machines, mechanical ideas and practical projects. It was the sort of reading that could make you look at an ordinary object and start wondering how it had been designed.

I was not simply interested in looking at the finished product. I wanted to understand what was inside it.

How did it work?

Why was it built that way?

Could I make something similar?

Could I improve it?

Those questions were becoming part of the way I looked at the world.

A technical book can do more than transfer information. It can introduce a person to possibilities they have never considered. For me, those books helped turn curiosity into imagination.

And imagination, as I would eventually discover, is often the first step towards creating something.

## 🏎️ The Electric Soapbox Dream.

One of the ideas that captured my imagination was building a soapbox car.

But I was not thinking of an ordinary soapbox car.

I wanted to use a starter motor as the drivetrain.

That was the sort of thinking that occupied my young mind. I was looking at a familiar childhood idea and imagining how it could be transformed through mechanical and electrical ingenuity.

A starter motor was a component designed for a specific purpose. But I was already wondering whether it could be used to make something else move.

It was not a carefully engineered project with a completed design, a parts list and a workshop waiting for me. It was an idea — an ambitious little engineering dream in the mind of a boy.

Yet that idea says something about the way I was beginning to think.

I was not satisfied with simply accepting that an object had one intended use. I wanted to explore what else might be possible.

That does not mean every idea was practical, or that I had the knowledge and resources to build everything I imagined. I was still learning.

But the important thing was that I was asking questions.

And I was beginning to think like a builder.

## 📷 Curiosity Meets a Camera.

My curiosity was not limited to things I read about.

I wanted to investigate real objects, too.

On one occasion, I decided to dismantle one of my father's cameras to find out how it worked.

I was interested in what was inside it. I wanted to see the mechanism for myself rather than simply accept that the camera performed a particular function.

So I took it apart.

There was just one small complication.

Putting it back together proved considerably more difficult than taking it apart.

As I remember the incident, nobody managed to reassemble it.

In retrospect, that little episode is rather amusing. I had successfully satisfied my curiosity about the camera's internal workings, but I had not necessarily improved the family's photographic equipment!

🔍 There is a difference between taking something apart and understanding it well enough to restore it. I was learning that difference the hard way.

Still, I would rather remember the incident for what it reveals about my curiosity than simply for the camera that did not make it back together.

I wanted to know.

I wanted to see.

I wanted to understand.

Sometimes the best way I could think of doing that was to open something up and investigate.

## 🧠 The Beginning of a Technical Mindset.

It would be easy to look at these childhood experiences and claim that they predicted everything that came later.

That would be overstating the matter.

A boy who reads technical magazines, dreams about building an electric soapbox car and dismantles a camera does not automatically become an engineer, programmer or entrepreneur.

Life is not that straightforward.

But I can recognise a connection between those early interests and the person I eventually became.

I have always enjoyed learning. I like discovering how things work, examining ideas and understanding the principles behind a system.

More importantly, I have never been particularly interested in knowledge that exists only to be collected. I want to apply what I learn when circumstances, opportunities and resources allow.

That distinction would become increasingly important in my adult life.

The curiosity was there first. The opportunities to put it to practical use would come later.

## 🚂 From the Family Library to the Real World.

My father's influence and the technical material available at home helped establish an early interest. But childhood imagination would eventually have to meet the realities of working life.

My later railway apprenticeship and working experience would give me opportunities to develop practical technical skills.

There is a significant difference between reading about a machine and working with real equipment.

Books can explain principles. Diagrams can show how components fit together. But practical work introduces responsibility, physical limitations, faults, procedures and the consequences of getting something wrong.

It is where theory meets reality.

The railway years would become an important part of my technical development, and they deserve their own place in this story. They were not the beginning of my curiosity, but they helped give that curiosity a more practical direction.

The boy who had once imagined an electrically powered soapbox car was beginning to encounter the real world of engineering and technical work.

## 🔑 What I Carried Forward.

When I look back at that young boy, I do not see someone who had already planned his future.

I see someone who was curious, fascinated by machines and eager to understand things.

My father gave me an example of what technical knowledge could become. He also showed me, through his kindness and his faith, that knowledge and ability mean more when they are accompanied by a willingness to help others. His books introduced me to ideas. *Popular Mechanics* encouraged my imagination. The soapbox car represented the desire to create something. And the camera incident demonstrated that curiosity sometimes gets ahead of practical ability!

But those experiences left me with something valuable.

The willingness to investigate.

The desire to learn.

The belief that complicated things can be examined and understood.

And the satisfaction of discovering how something works.

Those qualities would follow me into railway engineering, business, computers, web development, writing and eventually the creation of PlebWare.

The technology would change. The tools would become more sophisticated. The world would move from mechanical devices to digital systems.

But the underlying question would remain much the same:

**How does it work, and what can I do with it?**

## 🧭 Looking Ahead — The Next Chapter.

A technical childhood does not tell the whole story of a person.

There was also a young man growing up, discovering independence, friendships, entertainment, ambitions and the many distractions that life places in front of us.

I was not a young man who spent every waking moment planning an engineering career. I enjoyed life, too. I had interests beyond technical books and machinery.

The next chapter will look at that younger version of me: the reserved and sometimes rebellious young man, the ambitions I carried, the things I enjoyed, and the experiences that shaped my personality.

The engineer's son was only the beginning.
