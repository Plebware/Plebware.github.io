# 🔧 Otto’s Technical Autobiography.

## My Development Journals.

*The human story behind projects, experiments, ideas, and things being built.*

Welcome to my Development Journals, where I document the human side of creating, experimenting, learning, and solving problems.

These journals are not intended to replace my formal technical documentation. Instead, they record my personal experiences while developing projects, testing ideas, overcoming challenges, and discovering where a little ingenuity can lead.

This first entry is a technical autobiography, looking back at some of the projects I can still remember at the age of 63. I've selected a handful of projects from different stages of my life and across several disciplines, including electronics, carpentry, plumbing, computer coding, and even confectionery.

Although confectionery might not traditionally be considered a technical discipline, it certainly has its own technical challenges. Planning, preparation, timing, precision, and a little creativity all play their part.

Looking back, I realise that these projects share a common thread: identifying a problem, working out a solution, and then building something to make that solution a reality.

Let's take a trip down memory lane.

## 📡 1. The Wired Electronic Bug.

At approximately sixteen years of age, I decided to bug our living room using my 150-in-1 electronic kit, which I had received for my birthday the previous year.

Building the circuit was relatively straightforward. All I had to do was follow the circuit diagram supplied with the kit. The real challenge was installing the wiring without being noticed.

I prepared the cable by stretching a double strand of enamelled copper wire. I ran it along the side of the house through the garden, twisting the wire using a hand-operated drilling machine. The installation was completed in three stages over two days.

**Stage One: The Kitchen Speaker.**

I began by installing an extension speaker in the kitchen. While doing so, I routed the wire from the living room towards the speaker, concealing the spare length in the kitchen for the next stage.

**Stage Two: The Kitchen Walls.**

I volunteered to wash the kitchen walls, which gave me the perfect opportunity to work along the corners and ceiling.

Carefully, I routed the wire up the wall and along the cornice joint, securing it with Prestik so that it blended into the surroundings. From there, I continued the route towards the pantry.

**Stage Three: The Final Connection.**

Finally, I passed the wire through an air vent from the pantry into my bedroom.

Mission accomplished! Or so I thought.

The considerable length of wire connected to the small condenser microphone introduced an unexpected challenge. I had to adjust some resistor values to obtain the correct amplification.

Eventually, I got it working.

There was, however, one very important lesson still waiting for me — and it had nothing to do with electronics.

One day, Mum came to use the bathroom while we had visitors in the house. The bug picked up what it was never supposed to pick up, and she realised what I had been doing. She told Dad, who came straight to my room. He confiscated my electronic kit, and I received a severe punishment for that stunt.

Looking back, I can laugh at the memory, but the lesson was serious enough. I had concentrated on whether I could build the circuit and hide the wiring, without properly considering the privacy of the people in our home. Technical ability does not automatically make an idea a good one — and just because something can be built does not mean it should be used that way.

That was a memorable lesson in consequences, and certainly a more dramatic end to the experiment than I had planned!

## 💧 2. The Garden Irrigation System.

At the age of 24, I received a familiar complaint from my mother: watering the garden was tedious, and I wasn't helping enough!

Rather than simply promise to do better, I decided to tackle the problem with a little plumbing and engineering.

My solution was to divide the garden into four independently controlled zones using four ball-type lever cocks:

- The front garden.
- The main vegetable garden.
- The fruit trees.
- The backyard lawn.

The project required more than simply connecting a few pipes. I had to thread steel pipes and fabricate a metal bracket system to support the shut-off cocks and distribution pipes. The actual irrigation network was constructed using PVC piping.

I purchased twelve half-pattern pop-up sprinklers and six full-circle, 360-degree pop-up sprinklers.

Working at the South African Railways during the week meant that the project had to fit around my employment. It took three weekends to complete.

One particularly important consideration was protecting the main garden pipes. I buried them approximately three feet underground and used riser pipes to bring the connections back up to a convenient, serviceable height.

There was a practical reason for doing this: our gardener had a habit of accidentally chopping through pipes with his spade while working the soil.

The deeper installation helped protect the main supply lines from future damage.

In the end, I had a garden irrigation system divided into manageable zones, with the controls and plumbing arranged to suit the layout of the property.

It was a good example of how a little planning could turn a tedious household chore into a practical engineering project.

## 🪚 3. My First Entertainment Unit.

At the age of 25, I built my first piece of furniture.

Our living room had become cluttered with a cathode-ray-tube television, a new Hi-Fi player, and a VHS video recorder. The television already sat on a corner unit, so I decided to build an additional entertainment unit to accommodate the equipment neatly.

The idea was simple: make better use of the available space while giving the various components a proper home.

I built the unit from Supawood, cutting the material on my table saw in the garage.

From planning the dimensions to cutting and assembling the pieces, the project took me one weekend.

It was the first piece of furniture I had ever made, and completing it gave me the satisfaction of turning an idea into something useful with my own hands.

That first attempt also introduced me to the practical considerations of furniture construction: measurements, material selection, cutting accuracy, and making sure the finished piece served its intended purpose.

It might not have been a grand piece of cabinetry, but it was my first.

And everyone has to start somewhere!

## ⚡ 4. The Electronic Test Station.

My interest in electronics continued to grow, and as I acquired more testing and repair equipment, I realised that I needed a proper workbench.

Rather than settle for an ordinary table, I designed a seven-foot-long floating workbench.

The structure was built from square steel tubing, with a plywood top covered by a rubber conveyor-belt surface. I also incorporated an angled rear panel to accommodate the electrical controls, instruments, and connections I needed for my work.

The station included:

- Circuit breakers and 220-volt plug points.
- A 0–280-volt Variac for adjustable AC voltage.
- Three home-built adjustable DC power supplies: 0–30 volts, 0–20 volts, and 0–50 volts.
- A built-in car radio.
- Two 12-volt lead-acid batteries.
- A swivel-mounted work light.

My oscilloscope was mounted separately on a wall-mounted swivel arm, allowing me to position the instrument where it was most convenient.

The result was a dedicated electronics workspace, designed around the equipment I owned and the work I wanted to do.

Building the test station was about more than making a bench. It was about creating an environment where I could test circuits, investigate faults, experiment with power supplies, and work more efficiently.

In a way, I was designing my own little electronics laboratory.

## 🍳 5. The Kitchen Utility Pegboard.

My mother and I both enjoyed cooking, and our kitchen contained a growing collection of small utensils.

Spoons, ladles, sieves, a garlic press, graters, a pizza cutter, a rolling pin, and numerous other implements all needed somewhere to live.

My solution was to build a kitchen utility board that would keep these items organised and within easy reach.

I used a picture frame and pegboard as the foundation, then fashioned hooks from old wire coat hangers.

To finish the job, I painted the board with enamel paint to help protect it against moisture and the steam produced during cooking.

It was a straightforward project, but it solved a genuine household problem.

Instead of rummaging through drawers whenever we needed a particular utensil, we could arrange the tools where they were visible and accessible.

This was another example of a principle that has followed me through many different projects: **a useful solution does not have to be complicated; it simply has to work well.**

## 🎂 6. A Three-Tier, Heart-Shaped Wedding Cake.

Not all my technical adventures involved electronics, pipes, or timber.

When a friend got married, I decided to bake their wedding cake. And naturally, I decided to go all out!

I hired three heart-shaped baking tins and prepared the fruit cake several months in advance.

But baking the cake was only part of the undertaking. I also needed a way to decorate it properly, so I constructed my own icing stand.

The stand was made using a wooden cable bobbin, a set of ball bearings, and a round plywood disc. The cable bobbin may even have been the same one that had supplied the wire for my earlier electronic project!

The arrangement allowed me to turn the cake while applying the icing and decorations.

I crafted orchids, roses, and other flowers from sugar paste, adding decorative details to give the cake a more elaborate finish.

The fruit cake was regularly fed with brandy and rum, once or twice a week, as it matured.

When the time came to finish the cake, I covered it with a layer of marzipan followed by a layer of plastic icing. Royal icing was then used to create frills and delicate lacework around the edges.

The finished cake combined several different skills: baking, planning, construction, decoration, and patience.

It also demonstrated something I have always enjoyed about making things. Sometimes, the tools and techniques developed in one area of life turn out to be surprisingly useful in another.

Who would have thought that an interest in practical construction would help me build a cake-decorating stand?

## 🚗 7. The Talking Car.

Of all my electronic projects, one of the most ambitious was the Talking Car, which I designed in 1999, when I was 36.

This was no simple off-the-shelf alarm installation. I designed and built the entire alarm system from scratch.

The system incorporated an adjustable microwave detection field, a piezoelectric shock sensor, door-trigger inputs, and the other components required for a comprehensive vehicle alarm.

The microwave detector was particularly interesting because it could be adjusted to distinguish between ordinary passers-by and someone approaching the vehicle closely enough to warrant a warning.

I managed to tune the detection field so that someone merely walking past the car would not trigger the alarm.

However, if someone leaned towards the side window, bringing their nose to within approximately two or three inches of the glass, the system would activate a warning.

That warning was delivered through four horn speakers mounted in the wheel arches.

The idea was to tell the person to step away from the vehicle before escalating to a full alarm response.

If the person moved away and complied with the warning, the system would return to standby.

I also incorporated a simple indication system. When the alarm was disarmed, a single indicator LED would flash five times, accompanied by five audible chirps, to indicate that the microwave detector had registered someone lingering close to the vehicle.

This provided a record of a potential security incident without necessarily requiring the full alarm to sound.

The Talking Car represented a substantial step forward from the electronic experiments of my youth. It required me to combine circuit design, sensor adjustment, signal processing, practical installation, and the integration of several different functions into one working system.

Most importantly, it was a project in which I could develop the complete solution myself, rather than merely assemble a commercially available product.

## 🧠 What These Projects Taught Me.

Looking back at these seven projects, I can see a pattern that stretches across decades.

The electronic bug taught me to follow a circuit diagram, solve practical installation problems, and adjust a circuit until it worked as intended. It also taught me, rather more painfully, that technical curiosity must be balanced with respect for other people.

The irrigation system taught me to plan around the physical environment and anticipate problems before they became expensive repairs.

The entertainment unit and electronic test station taught me to design and construct equipment around a specific purpose.

The kitchen pegboard reminded me that even a simple improvement can make everyday life easier.

The wedding cake taught me patience, precision, and the value of creating the right tools for a particular task.

And the Talking Car brought many of my electronic skills together in one ambitious project.

These were different projects, built at different times, using different materials and techniques. Yet the underlying approach remained remarkably consistent.

First, identify the problem.

Then, understand what the solution must accomplish.

Work out how the pieces fit together, acquire the necessary materials, build the solution, test it, and make adjustments where needed.

Sometimes the result is successful on the first attempt. Sometimes it requires a little troubleshooting. And sometimes, the process teaches you something you never expected to learn.

That, to me, is the real joy of development.

## 🔭 Looking Ahead.

These recollections are only a small selection of the projects I have worked on throughout my life. There are undoubtedly others that will come back to me as I continue writing these journals.

My interests have evolved over the years, from electronic circuits and practical construction to computers, software, coding, and the development of ideas that can be shared with other people.

Today, I continue to explore those interests through my work on PlebWare and PlebMachine.

The tools may have changed, but the underlying curiosity remains the same.

I still want to understand how things work, discover what can be improved, and find practical ways to turn ideas into reality.

Perhaps that is the real story behind a lifetime of projects: not a collection of finished objects, but an ongoing willingness to learn, experiment, and build.

And at 63, I am not finished yet!
